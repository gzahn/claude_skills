# Mutualistic Arbuscular Endomycorrhizal Fungi (AM Fungi / Glomales)

> Source: Foster, Bills & Mueller (2004) Ch. 15 (Mutualistic Arbuscular Endomycorrhizal Fungi — Morton, Koske, Stürmer & Bentivenga).

---

## Overview

Arbuscular mycorrhizal (AM) fungi (Order Glomales, Phylum Zygomycota; ~163 species in 7 genera) form the most widespread mycorrhizal association among land plants (~80% of plant species). **Critical limitation: AM fungi cannot be cultured apart from their plant hosts.** The plant provides carbon via an arbuscule–plasmalemma interface; the fungus enhances phosphorus acquisition for the plant.

**Key taxa:** *Glomus* (91 spp.), *Acaulospora* (35 spp.), *Scutellospora* (28 spp.), *Gigaspora* (5 spp.), *Entrophospora* (4 spp.), *Archaeospora* (2 spp.), *Paraglomus* (2 spp.)

**Spore size range:** 20–800 µm diameter (complex subcellular wall layers diagnostic for species identification — requires differential interference contrast optics)

## Field Collection and Soil Sampling

### Collection Protocol (McKenney & Lindsey 1987 / INVAM standard)

1. Visual inspection of soil for epigeous sporocarps at collection site
2. Collect soil to 15–20 cm depth, ensuring sample contains all available roots
3. Mix sample with water in a blender for 30 seconds
4. Pass slurry through nested sieves with **500-, 250-, and 38- (or 45-) µm openings** using water spray
5. Examine material from 500- and 250-µm sieves for small sporocarps and large spores
6. Centrifuge material from 38- (or 45-) µm sieve in **20% and 60% sucrose density gradients** (50-ml tubes; swinging bucket rotor for optimal separation of soil particulates and spores)
7. Pour supernatant from each tube into 38- (or 45-) µm sieve; wash 1–2 minutes; rinse with distilled water; place in glass Petri dish for storage

**Alternatives:**
- Wash sievings through pre-wetted filter paper in Buchner funnel under vacuum — filter paper can be stored in Petri dishes at 4°C for 5–7 days, or dried and stored longer; rewet 24 h before counting
- Cut 2.5-cm diameter PVC pipe into 2-cm sections with nylon mesh on one end for custom sieves (Tetko, Inc. supplies various mesh sizes)

### Spore Counting

- Low spore numbers: count directly under stereomicroscope in gridded Petri dish
- High spore numbers: agitate Petri dish to distribute evenly; count spores from 30–40 randomly chosen grid cells; average and multiply by total grid cells
- Alternatively: place spores on gridded filter paper (eliminates movement; better for similar morphotypes)

### Sampling Design

- **Monthly sampling over 1–2 years** is most desirable; quarterly sampling more practical
- Single-date samples may not accurately represent fungal community (different species have unique seasonal patterns)
- Areas with low sporulation: also set up trap cultures to recover nonsporulating species
- Sites of low species richness: collect large numbers of samples (species-increment curves often plateau after 10–15 samples in productive habitats)

## Root Staining (Assessing Mycorrhizal Colonization)

**Clearing and staining protocol (Koske & Gemma 1989 — safer than phenol-trypan blue):**
1. Clear root cells of cytoplasm and acidify
2. Differentially stain fungal cell walls

**Staining options:**
- Trypan blue in lactic acid + phenol (classic; phenol hazardous)
- Trypan blue in lactic acid alone — safer equivalent
- Acid fuchsin (Kormanik et al. 1980)
- **Chlorazol black E** (Brundrett et al. 1984) — highest contrast among fungal structures

**Note:** A few plant species (*Allium*, *Zea mays*, *Hordeum vulgare*, *Triticum aestivum*) develop yellow pigments in colonized root areas visible without staining.

**Reference:** Sylvia (1994) provides detailed procedures for clearing/staining and estimating percentage mycorrhizal root length.

## Pot Cultures (Only Way to Culture AM Fungi)

### Overview

AM fungi **must be cultured in greenhouse pots on host plants** to obtain inocula of single isolates. Pot cultures serve as trap cultures (to bait sporulating and nonsporulating fungi from field roots) and as experimental microcosms.

**Requirements:**
- Low phosphorus growth medium (high phosphorus inhibits mycorrhizal development)
- Temperature, soil pH, moisture approximating conditions to which fungi are adapted
- Optimum light conditions to maximize photosynthetic carbon allocation to roots

### Culture Contamination Prevention (Critical)

1. No field soils or other potted plants in culture rooms (primary source of insect problems)
2. Remove surface dust; disinfect all surfaces at regular intervals
3. Handle pots carefully; avoid touching contents
4. Watering methods that prevent splashing and water movement between pots
5. Immediate chemical control response plan for insects (fungal gnats, Collembola are especially destructive)
6. Monitor with sentinel pots (sterile soil + host plant) placed randomly on culture tables

### Choosing Host Plant

- **Sudangrass (*Sorghum sudanense*)** — INVAM standard; supports colonization of >1000 isolates of 98 species (all genera)
- **Big bluestem (*Andropogon gerardii*)** — superior at some locations (University of Minnesota)
- **Bahiagrass (*Paspalum notatum*)** — effective in subtropical Florida
- C₄ grasses are generally most compatible and productive (abundant root system; efficient carbon partitioning)

### Growth Medium

- **Sand** — inert; requires daily fertilizer monitoring
- **Sand + low-phosphorus loamy soil (1:1 v/v)** — INVAM standard; adjusted to pH 6.2; requires fewer fertilizer applications
- **Sand + vermiculite** — also effective

### Pot Size

- Volumes <300 cm³: rarely induce sporulation of more than 1–2 species
- **1000–1500 cm³**: most diverse sporulation within 4–5 months
- Larger volumes: similar results but require longer culture periods

### Establishing Trap-Pot Cultures

1. Collect at least 250 cm³ of field soil with abundant roots; chop roots into small fragments; mix thoroughly with coarse sterilized sand (1:1 v/v) to fill pot of ≥600 cm³ volume
2. Sow host seeds; grow for minimum 4 months with fertilizer applied only when plants show N deficiency (yellow leaves) or P deficiency (red leaves/stems)
3. Harvest when shoots cease growth (30–40 days post-cessation = peak sporulation — photosynthetic carbon reallocates to spores at this time)
4. Take root samples for mycorrhizal colonization estimates; extract spores to measure abundance
5. If colonization and spore abundance are high: stop watering; dry pots slowly in cooler, shaded area; store in bags at 4°C (temperate isolates) or room temperature (tropical isolates) for minimum 30 days before extracting spores (accommodates spore dormancy)
6. If spore numbers are low: second propagation cycle — remove shoots with scalpel; reseed same pot; maintain 3–4 more months

### Culturing Species from Trap Cultures (INVAM Protocol)

1. Extract spores from stored cultures; group similar morphotypes (color, size, other macromorphology); mount minimum 10 spores on slides to verify identical morphology (these serve as permanent vouchers)
2. Place 50–100 spores of each morphotype in watchglass; remove all extraneous material (hyphae, root fragments, organic matter) with pipette; store at 4°C for ≥24 h; remove unhealthy or hyperparasitized spores
3. Place pot with 10–12 day-old host seedlings (sorghum preferred — robust roots, minimal transplantation shock) in sterilized glass bowl with water; gently tease roots apart
4. Remove 3 seedlings; wash away remaining soil/sand with gentle water stream; allow roots to entwine
5. Pipette spores from microtiter well directly along entwined roots (excess water drips without dislodging spores)
6. Plant seedlings in 3.8 × 21-cm cone-tainers (Stuewe and Sons) with sterile growth medium
7. Grow at least 90 days; examine for mycorrhizae and sporulation

**For single-spore cultures:** Pipette single spore onto roots at location midway along the root

## Spore Storage and Archives

**Living specimens:**
- Store pot cultures at 4°C (temperate isolates) or room temperature (tropical isolates) in sealed bags, preferably slightly moist
- INVAM (West Virginia) — oldest and largest collection: ~1100 accessions, >86 species; extensive website
- BEG (Bank of European Glomales) — network of researcher-maintained cultures registered at one central location
- Regional collections: University of Western Australia (Lynette Abbott); Instituto de Ecología y Sistemática Havana (Ricardo Herrera); Taiwan Agricultural Research Institute (Chi Guang Wu)

**Preserved specimens:**
- Spores mounted on glass slides can serve as permanent vouchers
- Filter paper with extracted spores (dried) suitable for medium-term storage
- Spores in water in plastic Petri dishes: short-term storage at 4°C (5–7 days)

## Identification

**Requires dissecting microscope** for macroscopic properties; compound microscope with differential interference contrast (DIC) optics for subcellular wall layers (<1 µm thick, often colorless).

Spore wall characteristics (layers, ornamentation, flexibility) are primary diagnostic features. Host plant identity does not indicate fungal identity (AM fungi have very low host specificity).

**Spore condition in field soils:** In high-organic/high-microbial-activity soils (especially tropics), most spores are too degraded or parasitized for reliable identification — use pot cultures instead.

**Key reference:** INVAM website (invam.caf.wvu.edu) — descriptions, images, keys for all known species.

## Cautions

1. **Cannot culture in absence of host** — all requests to "grow AM fungi on agar" will fail. Pot culture on host plant is the only method.
2. **Spore dormancy:** Allow 30 days minimum at 4°C storage before using extracted spores as inocula
3. **Contamination monitoring:** Maintain sentinel control pots; dust during harvest is the most persistent contamination source — carry out setup/harvest in separate room; mist air when handling cultures
4. **Intraradical sporulators:** Use of roots-only inoculum can enrich cultures with species that sporulate inside roots (*Glomus clarum*, *G. diaphanum*, *G. intraradices*), causing abnormal dominance
