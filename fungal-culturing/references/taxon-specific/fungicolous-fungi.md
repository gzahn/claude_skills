# Fungicolous Fungi (Mycoparasites and Sporocarp-Inhabiting Fungi)

> Source: Foster, Bills & Mueller (2004) Ch. 17 (Fungicolous Fungi — Gams, Diederich & Põldmaa).

---

## Overview

"Fungicolous fungi" are species consistently associated with other fungi as **mycoparasites** (one living fungus serves as nutrient source for another), **commensals** (regular association with little visible effect on host), or saprobes on dead fungal tissue. Lichenicolous fungi (on lichens) are treated as a subgroup.

**Estimated species diversity:** ~1,700 non-lichenized species (Rudakov 1978); ~1,100 conidial species on ~2,500 host taxa (Hawksworth 1981). Lichenicolous fungi: >1,500 known, probably >3,000 total.

**"SCIF"** = Sporocarp-Inhabiting Fungi (fungicolous fungi on macromycete or myxomycete sporocarps specifically).

---

## Types of Associations

**Biotrophic mycoparasitism:** Host cytoplasm remains healthy (at least initially); specialized infection structures (haustoria); nutrient uptake from living host cells. Generally more host-specific than necrotrophic.

**Necrotrophic mycoparasitism:** Host cytoplasm degenerates; hyphal lysis. Typically releases toxins and lytic enzymes (especially chitinase). Less host-specific; broader host range.

**Semi-biotrophic:** Biotrophic initially → necrotrophic later; common intermediate condition.

**Commensalism:** Regular association with host fungus; no visible damage.

**Mycoparasite–host interface types (Jeffries & Young 1994):**
1. **Contact necrotrophic:** Hyphae don't penetrate; damage by hyphal interference (*Arthrobotrys superba*, *Tilletiopsis* on powdery mildews)
2. **Invasive necrotrophic:** Hyphae penetrate and grow within host cells (*Talaromyces*, *Trichoderma*, many others)
3. **Haustorial (biotrophic):** Short haustorial branch penetrates host hypha (zygomycete mycoparasites, *Tremellales*, *Sporidesmium sclerotivorum*)
4. **Fusion (biotrophic):** Micropores develop in contact walls — cytoplasmic contact without full penetration (*Gonatobotrys simplex*, *Melanospora zamiae*)
5. **Intracellular:** Complete thallus enters host hypha (many chytrids and oomycetes)

**Hyphal interference:** Growth reduction and cytoplasmic disruption of one fungus by proximity (within ~50 µm) or contact with another, without penetration. Observed in dual culture.

## Detection in the Field

**SCIF on macromycete sporocarps:**
- Usually detectable from macroscopically visible deformations and color changes of host
- Tiny perithecia and conidia (e.g., Hypocreales on Aphyllophorales) easily overlooked
- To detect fungi inside perennial polypore hymenium: cut sporocarps open
- Slime mold parasites: look for sporocarps on infected myxomycete fruiting bodies
- Seasonality: agaricicolous and boleticolous *Hypomyces* peak in autumn (temperate)
- Early season anamorphs on fresh sporcarps → ascomata on significantly decayed or adjacent substrata (bark, mosses)

**Lichenicolous fungi:**
- Examine many thalli of different lichen species; concentrate on abnormal-looking, damaged, or dying lichens
- Many species grow on healthy thalli without visible damage
- Hand lens detects most species in field; some intrahymenial hyphomycetes and heterobasidiomycetes require microscopy

## Collection Methods

**SCIF (Sporocarp-Inhabiting Fungi):**
- Same methods as macromycete collection (see macrofungi.md and soil-fungi.md for general guidance)
- Collect both infected and asymptomatic sporocarps
- When pure cultures cannot be prepared in field: air-dry infected material; isolate after return to lab (even months later); conidial material keeps better in cool place; dried conidia lose germination capability faster
- Sections of deformed host fungi not showing visible parasite: incubate in moist chamber to promote mycoparasite sporulation and ascoma ripening
- Transfer intact remnants of host onto agar before purifying — can activate some biotrophic mycoparasites

**Lichenicolous fungi:**
- Normal lichen collection techniques (see lichens.md)
- Separate specimens with unusual growth/damage; label clearly; send to specialists
- Major host groups especially rich in lichenicolous fungi: *Cladonia*, *Lobaria*, *Parmelia* s.l., *Peltigera*, *Pertusaria*, *Pseudocyphellaria*, *Usnea*
- Macrolichens richer hosts than crustose lichens

## Isolation and Culture

**Standard media for SCIF:**
- Cornmeal agar (CMA; transparent, nutritionally poor) — preferred by many investigators; with 2% dextrose or oatmeal agar as variants
- Malt extract agar (MEA) or MEA + peptone
- Potato-dextrose agar (PDA)
- Czapek agar + yeast and malt extracts (Rudakov 1981)
- **Hypomyces fruiting agar** (mixture of PDA and MEA): enhances perithecial production of *Hypomyces polyporinus* (Carey & Rogerson 1981)

**Improved sporulation:**
- **Mushroom/host-derived agars:** mushroom extract agar; homogenized and sterilized carpophores of various host fungi (e.g., Russula agar; de Hoog 1978)
- **Polypore extract agar:** 200 g polypore flour infused with 1 liter distilled water; OR 1% glucose agar with gas-sterilized pieces of polypore blocks
- CMA or dilute media (potato-carrot agar) prevent rapid deterioration of *Cladobotryum* anamorphs during long-term maintenance (preferred over rich media for storage)

**Isolation from sporulating material:**
- Transfer small amounts of conidia or ascospores with fine (glass) needle to media containing antibiotics → usually yields pure cultures directly or after a few transfers
- Old/decaying polypore sporocarps may host several fungicolous fungi simultaneously: streak dilute spore suspension from hymenium surface to separate colonies

**Teleomorph induction (for heterothallic species):**
- Reliable isolation from germinated ascospores (preferably single spores) required to establish anamorph-teleomorph connections
- For heterothallic *Hypomyces*: mate compatible isolates; usually successful in fresh ascosporic isolates

**Lichenicolous fungi:**
- Normally studied from herbarium specimens using light microscopy (water, KOH, lactophenol-cotton blue mounts)
- Culturing possible but not routine in lichenology — growing interest since 1990s
- Fungal isolates obtained from *Cladonia* and *Stereocaulon* in Germany included soil/litter fungi, plant symbionts/pathogens, and true lichenicolous fungi

## Determining Mode of Interaction (Lab Methods)

**Visual observation of infection structures:**
- Water, lactic acid + cotton blue, or other standard mountants for microscopic observation
- Phloxine stain (dilute): dead cells absorb dye immediately; living cells remain unstained for several minutes → distinguishes living from dead host cells
- Immunolabeled dyes: locate specific organisms
- Infrared photomicrography: detect sites of strong enzymatic activity
- TEM: detailed ultrastructure of haustoria, penetration pegs, callosities

**Dual culture studies:**
- Grow potential antagonist on cellophane disk on PDA/other medium for several days; remove cellophane; inoculate test fungus in same spot → assess degree of inhibition
- Volatile metabolite effects: fasten plates inoculated with host and antagonist opposite each other in Petri dish
- Slide culture technique (Lutchmeah & Cooke 1984): coat large coverslips with water agar; inoculate opposite sides with blocks from target fungi; observe interactions at 70× oil immersion for 1–1.5 hours

**Radioisotope tracing:** ³²P transfer from host (*Rhizoctonia solani*) to parasite (*Arthrobotrys oligospora*) demonstrates nutrient flow definitively.

## Key Taxonomic Groups and Hosts

| Host group | Common fungicolous taxa |
|---|---|
| Boletes, Paxillus | *Hypomyces chrysospermus* (anamorph *Sepedonium chrysospermum*) — very visible golden spores covering entire host |
| Agarics (general) | *Hypomyces* spp. (55+ species); Tremellales (*Tremella*, *Syzygospora*); *Asterophora* |
| Aphyllophorales (polypores) | *Hypomyces polyporinus*; *Cladobotryum* spp.; various pyrenomycetes |
| Gasteromycetes, Phallales | *Sepedonium* spp.; various mold genera |
| Myxomycetes | *Hypomyces* spp.; species of *Hyphomycetes* (treated by Rogerson & Stephenson 1993) |
| Powdery mildews (Erysiphales) | *Ampelomyces quisqualis*; *Lecanicillium muscarium*; *Acremonium alternatum* |
| Rusts (Uredinales) | *Eudarluca caricis*; *Sphaerellopsis filum* |
| Plant pathogenic sclerotia | *Coniothyrium minitans*; *Sporidesmium sclerotivorum*; *Trichoderma* spp. |
| Lichens | Tremellales; various Ascomycota; Arthoniales; Hypocreales |
| Aquatic oomycetes/chytrids | Chytrid hyperparasites of *Phytophthora* oospores |

## Biocontrol Applications

Many fungicolous fungi are commercialized or studied as biocontrol agents:
- **Soil-borne pathogens:** *Trichoderma* spp. (widely commercialized); *Coniothyrium minitans* (*Sclerotinia* biocontrol); *Sporidesmium sclerotivorum* 
- **Powdery mildews:** *Ampelomyces quisqualis* (commercial product AQ10®)
- **Botrytis/Fusarium:** *Clonostachys rosea* (formerly *Gliocladium roseum*)
- **Mushroom crop pests:** *Cladobotryum* spp. ("cobweb disease") are themselves pests of mushroom crops

## Practical Notes for Inventory

- Occurrence of SCIF is even more erratic than host sporulation — requires extended search over multiple seasons
- **Quantitative census:** Requires regular observation of permanent plots; time-consuming; rarely done for SCIF compared to soil fungi
- Humid climates significantly richer in mycoparasites on both macromycetes and plant pathogens
- In Moscow region: highest species richness/frequency of SCIF recorded in mid-to-late summer (host abundance + dew deposition + airborne inoculum); succession: early *Hypomyces* anamorphs → *Calcarisporium*, *Acremonium* → *Mucor*, *Mortierella*, *Trichoderma*, *Penicillium*, *Alternaria*, *Cladosporium* on almost-decayed hosts
- Polypore carpophores infected: ~5% in Rudakov's (1981) study, mostly commensals
