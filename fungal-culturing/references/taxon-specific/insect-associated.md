# Insect- and Other Arthropod-Associated Fungi

> Source: Foster, Bills & Mueller (2004) Ch. 18 (Insect- and Other Arthropod-Associated Fungi — Benjamin et al.).

---

## Overview

Vast numbers of fungi form associations with insects and other arthropods: necrotrophic parasites (kill host cells), biotrophic parasites (require living host cells), mutualists (provide enzymes/nutrients), commensals, and incidental associates. Key groups:

- **Trichomycetes** — obligate gut parasites of mandibulate arthropods
- **Laboulbeniales** — ectoparasitic ascomycetes on arthropod exoskeleton
- **Necrotrophic parasites** — insect pathogens (*Beauveria*, *Metarhizium*, Entomophthorales, etc.)
- **Bark beetle associates** — ophiostomatalean fungi, yeasts, corticioid basidiomycetes; dispersed in mycangia
- **Ambrosia beetle fungi** — obligate mutualists (*Ambrosiella*, *Raffaelea*)
- **Attine ant and termite garden fungi** — *Leucoagaricus*, *Termitomyces*
- **Septobasidiales** — basidiomycete scale-insect parasites

---

## General Sampling Principles

- Knowledge of host insect biology is essential — many fungi associate only with larval stage during a single brief season; may be ephemeral in somatic and sporulation phases
- Target sampling methods at the host insects
- Quantitative sampling possible for fungi on living adult hosts; very difficult for fungi on dead/dying insects

**Handling and storage of collected material:**
- Place in paper bags, vials, or other suitable containers; do not use plastic bags unless necessary (aquatic samples)
- Keep specimens separate to avoid cross-contamination between hosts
- Refrigerate insects in ice-filled coolers for transport
- Keep material in as unchanged a condition as possible — some fungi die within hours of drying
- When culture is important: use fresh material; attempt cultivation in field if necessary
- Refrigeration: good for a few days, but high humidity in refrigerators promotes contamination by other fungi. Walk-in refrigerators can become incubators for saprobes
- Cooling/freezing may slow insects for examination; care with cold-intolerant species

## Insect Collection Methods for Fungal Study

### Flight-Interception Traps
- ~3 m across; black fine-mesh netting suspended vertically above trays of preservative
- Quantitative; not influenced by incidental variables
- Good for Coleoptera (especially Staphylinidae, Scolytinae, Platypodinae) and Diptera
- Useful for Laboulbenialean fungi infection patterns

### Light Traps
- UV and mercury vapor lamps
- Attract wide range including Coleoptera, Diptera, aquatic beetles (Gyrinidae, Dytiscidae, Hydrophilidae — rich source of Laboulbeniales)
- Between- and within-site comparisons possible; elevational transects feasible

### Pitfall Traps
- Plastic/metal containers sunk so lip is at soil surface
- Best for ground-dwelling beetles (Carabidae)
- Trap in grid system for predefined periods; quantitative

### Canopy Fogging
- Synthetic pyrethroid (Reslin E) fogged from ground; arthropods collected on suspended trays and plastic sheets
- Large numbers of beetles (Chrysomelidae, Corylophidae); hosts of Corylophomyces, Dimeromyces, Laboulbenia, Rickia (Laboulbeniales)

### Litter Samples
- Known volumes of litter through Berlese funnels
- Carabidae, Staphylinidae including Pselaphinae, Ptiliidae, ants, millipedes, mites

### Malaise Traps
- Useful for Trichomycetes in aquatic insects; catches can be standardized by trapping effort

## Trichomycetes (Obligate Gut Parasites)

**Hosts and habitats:**
- **Harpellales:** aquatic; in guts of dipteran larvae (chironomid midges, blackflies, mosquitoes, craneflies, biting midges, moth flies) and mayfly/stonefly nymphs; lotic streams preferred; smaller streams have greater diversity
- **Asellariales:** lotic and lentic isopods, amphipods, some beetles, springtails; freshwater, terrestrial, marine
- **Eccrinales:** millipedes, crabs, isopods, amphipods, anomurids; freshwater, terrestrial, marine (some deep-sea/hydrothermal vent species)
- **Amoebidiales:** water flea exoskeletons, mosquitoes, bloodworms, crayfish

**Collection:**
- Best collecting instrument: aquatic D-shaped net with fine mesh
- Kick-sampling: kick stream substrata (rocks, gravel) to release insects into drift
- Lift larger rocks; scrape with hand
- Collect from vegetation, sticks, small rocks; harvest attached insects with blunt forceps
- Large white plastic trays (40 × 30 cm) as field repositories for net/strainer contents
- Keep insects alive in shallow layers of water in wide-mouth jars; refrigerate in ice chest
- Marine crustaceans: collect by hand or net at low tide; mud flats (fiddler crabs, anomurids) — may need trowel/shovel; some deep-water forms require submersibles

**Dissection:**
- Most dissections require dissecting microscope
- Tools: two pairs fine jeweler's forceps; sharp single-edged razor blade; two fine dissecting needles; dissecting scissors (fine iris scissors)
- Mayflies/stoneflies: grasp posterior abdominal segment with forceps; pull to remove hindgut; remove epithelial layer in water drop; open gut to reveal Harpellales
- Dipteran larvae (midges, blackflies): detach posterior end and head; remove gut; fungi in hindgut attached to chitinous lining; midgut fungi on peritrophic membrane (remove by grasping one end and lifting several times through water surface)
- Isopods/amphipods: pull away posterior segment to remove hindgut
- Crabs/anomurids: cut away exoskeleton with scissors to reach hindgut and stomach (foregut)
- Millipedes: cut off posterior ~1/4 with razor blade

**Microscopy:** Mount in water (NOT fixative) first — fine details (trichospore appendages) may become impossible to discern in fixative. After study, infiltrate lactophenol + cotton blue from edge of coverslip; seal three sides with nail polish, let dry, seal fourth side.

### Trichomycete Cultivation

**Only some Harpellales can be cultured:** genera Capniomyces, Furculomyces, Genistelloides, Simuliomyces, Smittium, Trichozygospora. Most other Trichomycetes (all Eccrinales, Asellariales, Amoebidiales) cannot be cultured axenically.

**Isolation medium:** Dilute brain-heart infusion (**1/10 BHI** amended with thiamine and biotin; see media-recipes.md). Same medium used for refrigerator storage; generally produces good sporulation.

**Protocol:**
1. Dissect larva; remove hindgut (outer layers need not be removed if microscopy confirms fungus is present)
2. Wash hindgut at least twice in antibiotic wash: stock solution of 40,000 units penicillin G + 80,000 units streptomycin sulfate per ml distilled water (filter-sterilized; dispensed by syringe); 3–5 drops concentrate per 35 mm × 10 mm plastic Petri dish of wash water
3. Transfer washed gut to 60 mm × 15 mm Petri dish with thin layer of 1/10 BHI overlayered with sterile glass-distilled water + 3–5 drops antibiotic stock
4. Incubate at room temperature (most spp.); some cold-water stonefly species prefer ~18°C
5. Monitor daily with low-power compound microscope; if contamination appears, rewash and replate
6. Growth usually apparent in 2 days to 2 weeks
7. When growth evident, transfer to medium without antibiotics; later to test-tube slant with small amount (~20 mm deep) sterile distilled water added after agar gels
8. Rotate tube daily until some fungus adheres to agar (but usually doesn't grow into it)

## Bark Beetle-Associated Fungi

**Key taxa:** Ophiostomatales (Ophiostoma, Leptographium, Sporothrix), Microascales (Ceratocystis, Chalara), yeasts, corticioid basidiomycetes. Many are sapstain fungi (blue-stain fungi) or conifer pathogens.

**Mycangia:** Specialized structures (pits in exoskeleton of head, pronotum, elytra) used to store, culture, and transport fungi. Fungi in mycangia often yeast-like rather than hyphal.

### Collection

- Pitfall traps baited with turpentine + ethanol for root-feeding weevils/beetles
- Stem sections (billets) as baits in soil
- Flight traps baited with species-specific pheromones for upper-stem bark beetles
- **Critical:** Collect insects same day traps are deployed to avoid saprobic overgrowth; alternatively use electric cooler in collection cup to slow predators

### Storage

- Isolate fungi as soon after collection as possible
- Otherwise: store insects, mites, tree tissue in sterile vials on ice; refrigerate until processed
- Small pine twigs and/or moist paper in vials extend insect survival
- Keep insects in separate vials to minimize cross-contamination
- Tree tissue: refrigerate or store in moist chambers (promotes sporulation on host substratum)

### Isolation

**From mycangia (e.g., southern pine beetle pronotum):**
1. Remove pronotum from head and abdomen; remove legs with sterile fine forceps
2. Immerse intact pronotum in sterile distilled water (1 min) → modified White's solution (4 min) → two rinses in sterile distilled water
3. Quarter pronotum aseptically; place sections on MEA or MEA + 2 µg/ml benomyl (semiselective for *Ceratocystiopsis ranaculosus* and corticioid fungi)

**From whole beetles:**
- Crush beetle on agar medium OR grind in sterile distilled water in glass tissue homogenizer; plate dilutions on agar

**From gallery material:**
- Aseptically collect phloem/xylem from insect feeding sites, entrance holes, galleries, frass
- Place directly on water agar (WA)
- Transfer masses of spores from gallery-lining fruiting structures directly with fine needle

**Selective medium for ophiostomatalean fungi:** MEA, WA, or PDA + 200 ppm cycloheximide (ophiostomataleans highly tolerant) + 100 ppm streptomycin sulfate. Some yeasts and *Penicillium* may also grow.

**Standard incubation:** 20–25°C; some associates prefer cooler temperatures.

**Storage:** Cultures in weak agar in vials at –20°C or lyophilized. Avoid serial transfer on rich media.

## Ambrosia Beetle-Associated Fungi

**Primary symbionts:** *Ambrosiella* and *Raffaelea* (both sexual genus Ophiostoma-related); dimorphic (yeast-like in mycangia; filamentous in galleries; cropped by feeding beetles). In warmer regions *Fusarium* may also be an ambrosial associate.

**Secondary/auxiliary ambrosial fungi:** isolated from brood tunnels after beetle pupation; often have mucilaginous spores transmitted by phoretic mites and beetles. These slow-growing primary symbionts are easily overwhelmed in culture.

**Collection:** Same as bark beetle methods. During dispersal flight (northern temperate regions): short flight season; pheromone traps; emergence traps on logs.

**Isolation of primary symbionts:** Use fresh beetles; isolate immediately after collection. Selective cycloheximide medium helps. Dilution plating separates colonies.

## Attine Ants and Termites — Fungus Garden Cultivation

**Ant gardens:** Leafcutter ants (*Atta*, *Acromyrmex*) and other attine ants cultivate *Leucoagaricus* (Lepiotaceae) and related fungi in underground chambers. Fungus gardens are complex ecosystems with multiple fungal species.

**Termite gardens:** Some termite groups (*Macrotermes* and related genera in Africa/Asia) cultivate *Termitomyces* (Agaricales, Lyophyllaceae).

**Collection:** Collect fresh fungal garden material with sterile tools from the interior (interior material is least contaminated). Freeze or fix for vouchers. Use fresh material for cultures immediately.

**Culture media for ant/termite fungi:** Glucose, malt extract, and yeast extract agars; various modifications depending on species.

## Laboulbeniales (Ectoparasitic Ascomycetes on Arthropods)

These tiny, obligate parasites grow on arthropod exoskeleton. Cannot be cultured. Study requires permanent slide preparations.

**Collection:** Collect host arthropods by appropriate trapping method; examine exoskeleton under dissecting microscope; mount infected areas on slides.

**Permanent slides (double coverslip method):**
1. Remove small piece of exoskeleton bearing the fungus
2. Place in a drop of mounting medium on slide
3. Place small fragments of coverslip on four sides as spacers (make by breaking coverslips with thumb/finger pressure while holding against paper towel)
4. Lower second coverslip gently at 45° angle to avoid air bubbles
5. Seal with nail polish extending onto slide

---

## Septobasidiales

Basidiomycetes parasitic on scale insects; form hyphal mats over scale insect colonies on bark. Cannot be cultured easily. Study primarily from field collections and permanent mounts. Key references: Couch (1935), Azema (1975).

**Vouchers:** Deposit as dried specimens enclosed in packets or small boxes.
