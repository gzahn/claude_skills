# Yeasts — Isolation and Culturing

> Source: Foster, Bills & Mueller (2004) Ch. 16 (Yeasts — Kurtzman & Fell).

---

## Overview

Yeasts are a polyphyletic group (~100 genera, >700 described species as of 2004; likely <1% of species existing in nature) of ascomycetous and basidiomycetous fungi characterized by a unicellular growth phase. Sexual stages are not enclosed in fruiting bodies.

**Taxonomic note:** Molecular (rDNA) identification is now essential for accurate species identification — many phenotypic characters vary among strains of the same species. Molecular methods needed if large numbers of isolates are to be examined.

**Ecological note:** Yeast distributions are patchy; single-celled organisms aggregate in soils and sediments. Low-nutrient environments may harbor dormant cells that grow rapidly when organic material is encountered. Introduced species may not survive in soils.

---

## Distribution in Nature

| Habitat | Notes |
|---|---|
| Soil | Most species; upper layers (obligate aerobes); densities 10⁵–10⁶ CFU/g in productive soils |
| Leaf surfaces | 10⁵–10⁷ viable cells/g fresh foliage |
| Flowers, decaying fruits | High numbers; up to 10⁶ cells/g from decaying cactus cladodes |
| Plant exudates | Common; high densities |
| Insects | Very rich sources, especially wood-borers and *Drosophila* |
| Freshwater | Highest near land; lower in open water |
| Open ocean | Often as low as 10 cells/g; can increase to 10³ cells/g at plankton blooms, current boundaries, surface slicks, thermoclines |
| Marine, deep sea | Decreased with depth and distance from land |

**Exclusively soil-inhabiting genera:** *Debaryomyces* (*Schwanniomyces*) *occidentalis*, *Lipomyces* spp., *Schizoblastosporion starkeyi-henricii*, some *Cryptococcus* spp.

## Collection

- Most yeasts are obligate aerobes → found in **upper layers** of soil/sediment
- **Do not use airtight containers** — anaerobic conditions are deleterious to cell survival (containers should be closed adequately to prevent contamination, but not air-tight)
- **Process samples immediately** or hold on ice; low temperature critical for cold-habitat samples
- **Work in contaminant-free environment** if possible; avoid air drafts (primary contamination source); improvise temporary hoods in field
- Sterilize utensils by flaming with alcohol
- Maintain workspace clean with disinfectant

**Sample collection by habitat:**
- **Soil/shallow sediment:** sterile jars, vials, reclosable plastic bags with zippers, coring devices
- **Deep-water sediments:** remote grabs, gravity and piston cores, submersibles; section cores and sample at recorded depths
- **Deep-sea water:** Sterile Bag Sampler (General Oceanics) or Niskin bottles; 50 ml usually adequate; record temperature and salinity simultaneously; include minimal water to avoid anaerobic conditions

## Isolation Strategies

### Direct Plating

1. Place soil particles directly on agar medium; incubate ≥24 hours
2. Examine microscopically under low power to locate yeast growth at particle/medium interface
3. Transfer suspected yeast colonies to microscope slide for confirmation
4. Confirmed growths transferred from plate/slide to growth medium
5. Streak cultures to purify; single-cell colonies transferred to slant tubes

**For water samples:** Filter through 0.45-µm nitrocellulose filters; place filter face-up on solid nutrient agar with bacterial inhibitors; quantity filtered = few ml to 2–3 L depending on population density; enumerate and isolate colonies.

### Soil Dilution

- Mix known quantity of soil with known volume of sterile water (seawater for marine samples) in test tube
- Make serial dilutions; spread onto isolation media; enumerate as described in Ch. 13 suspension plating protocol

### Enrichment Culture

1. Place sample in flask with medium tailored to target yeast's nutritional preferences
2. Customize medium with: specific carbon/nitrogen compounds, vitamins, growth factors; or manipulate substratum osmolality (osmophilic species), salinity (marine species)
3. Incubate 12–24 hours (or more) with continuous shaking
4. Sample by streaking or spreading enrichment suspension onto isolation plates
5. Shake culture causes filamentous fungi to form pellets; yeasts remain free cells — highly selective

**Examples of enrichment conditions:**
- *Cyniclomyces guttulatus* (rabbit feces): amino acid medium + 35–40°C + elevated CO₂
- *Rhodosporidium lusitaniae* (forest soil): medium with lignin-related phenolic compounds as sole carbon source
- Osmophilic yeasts: adjust aW with glycerol, sucrose, or NaCl
- Marine yeasts: use seawater at 37‰; for inshore/estuarine, use 15‰ brackish water

**Incubation tip:** For samples from temperate/tropical areas, incubating at 12°C slows filamentous fungi relative to yeasts. **Examine plates daily** to isolate yeasts before overgrowth.

## Isolation Media

**Standard yeast isolation media:**
- **PYG (Peptone-Yeast-Glucose; also called Peptone-Yeast Extract-Glucose):** Most common isolation medium (see media-recipes.md)
- **Wickerham's YM medium (MEA + yeast extract):** Alternative; various carbon sources can be substituted for glucose

**Adaptations for aquatic samples:**
- Use freshwater or filtered seawater with either medium
- Ocean sampling: seawater at 37‰
- Inshore/estuarine: brackish water at 15‰
- Solidify with 2–3% agar

**Antibiotics:** Chloramphenicol 200 mg/L (added prior to autoclaving); OR penicillin G + streptomycin sulfate, each at 150–500 mg/L (added dry to autoclaved, cooled medium).

**pH adjustment:** Most yeasts tolerate pH 3.5–3.8; lowering pH can reduce bacteria but some bacteria grow at that pH range too — antibiotics are usually more effective.

## Practical Challenges

1. **Overgrowth by filamentous fungi:** Use shake culture (pellets filamentous fungi) or incubate at lower temperature (12°C slows filamentous fungi). Check plates daily.
2. **Bacterial contamination:** Add antibiotics to medium; pH reduction less reliable.
3. **"Weed" species overgrow rare ones:** Maximum ~300 colonies per plate; rare species at <1% of community will be missed or overgrown.
4. **All isolation media are selective:** Some dominant strains in soil may not grow on artificial media; standing crop (cell numbers) does not reflect turnover rates, hyphal fragmentation, spore release, or invertebrate consumption rates.
5. **Patchy distribution:** Single samples extrapolate to nearby samples only with caution. Multiple collections at each site required.

## Characterization

**Standard phenotypic description (still widely used):**
- Fermentation rates on 7–8 different sugars
- Growth rates on ~40 carbon sources and 1–2 nitrogen compounds (nitrate, lysine)
- Growth at various temperatures
- Morphology of vegetative growth and sexual stage
- DBB (diazonium blue B) test: usually discriminates ascomycetes (negative) from basidiomycetes (positive)

**Molecular characterization (now required for accurate species identification):**
- rDNA sequence comparison with reference strains
- D1/D2 domain of large subunit rRNA gene: most widely used for yeast species discrimination
- ITS region: additional resolution
- Gene scanner length polymorphisms: useful for rapid screening

## Preservation

**Key organisms (undescribed species or type strains) must be preserved.** Deposit in culture collections if identifications are accurate — fewer isolates need to be maintained if molecular ID is reliable.

**Major yeast culture collections:**
- ATCC (USA), NRRL (USA), CBS-Yeast Division (Netherlands), IFO and JCM (Japan), NCYC (UK), DSMZ (Germany), DBVPG (Italy), PYCC (Portugal), CCY and RIVE (Slovakia)

**Preservation methods:** See preservation-storage.md. Lyophilization works well for most sporulating yeasts. Liquid nitrogen for non-sporulating or fragile species.

## Field Data to Record

GPS coordinates; climate; soil type and properties; vegetation description; pH; salinity; water temperature (for aquatic samples); depth (for water column/sediment samples); date; collector name. Environmental data critical for understanding ecology and future comparisons given rapid human-generated environmental change.
