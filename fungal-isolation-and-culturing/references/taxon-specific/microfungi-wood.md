# Microfungi on Wood and Plant Debris

> Source: Foster, Bills & Mueller (2004) Ch. 11 (Microfungi on Wood and Plant Debris — Cannon & Sutton).

---

## Overview

"Microfungi" are defined by microscopic spore-producing structures — a polyphyletic assemblage including most Ascomycota (~32,250 spp.), Anamorphic Fungi/conidial fungi (~15,000 spp.), and some microscopic Basidiomycota. Most temperate and tropical plant debris surfaces harbor 5–30+ microfungal species simultaneously. Diversity is typically strongly host-associated for biotrophic and necrotrophic species; more substrate-type-associated (longevity, topography, water retention) for saprobes.

**Life strategies (after Grime 1977):**
- **Ruderal:** Short-lived, high reproductive output; disturbed/productive environments; sensitive to water stress; many Mucorales
- **Competitive:** Rapid or persistent growth; antagonistic resource defense; difficult to enumerate without selective methods
- **Stress-tolerant:** Endure stress; slow growth/reproduction; good enzymatic competence; persistent

**Major limitation:** Most microfungi are only identifiable from sporulating material. Many produce melanized resting structures (chlamydospores, sclerotia, bulbils) during unfavorable conditions and must be cultured to induce sporulation. Most species require compound microscopy with oil immersion and ideally Nomarski/DIC optics.

---

## Key Limitations for Sampling

1. **Cryptic nature:** Fruiting bodies as small as 50 µm diameter; conidiophores 10 µm long; spores 2–3 µm. Direct field observation rarely productive.
2. **Anamorph/teleomorph disconnect:** Many species have multiple morphs produced at different times; understanding life history requires both morphs be found and linked.
3. **Substrate-specific knowledge lacking:** Ecological preferences rarely documented; no comprehensive database of substrate preferences.
4. **Non-sporulating cultures:** Large proportions of isolates (~25%+) may not sporulate in culture; difficult to identify; use molecular methods or aggregate into "non-sporulating" categories.

## General Field Collection

**Equipment:** Sharp knife, clippers (secateurs), small axe, wood chisel, folding saw, good-quality hand lens; camera with ring-flash for field photography; small boxes (fishing tackle type) for fragile specimens.

**Containers:** Paper bags or glassine packets. **Never plastic** — uncontrolled humidity buildup promotes unwanted Mucorales overgrowth. Fragile specimens in small boxes.

**Field notes to record:**
- Identity of associated plant species; precise plant part colonized; date; location within site; microclimate (sun/shade, aspect)
- Prevailing weather conditions
- Population structure and health of host plant
- Degree of host decay (standardized scale if possible)

**Sampling strategy:**
- Collect samples showing visible signs of fungal growth; also some asymptomatic dead tissue for moist chamber studies
- Separate: fallen plant parts vs. dead attached material; tissue in various decay stages; tissue affected by animals
- Collect voucher material of host plant if identity not obvious
- For leaves: standardize sample size (defined piece including midrib and lamina); sample upper and lower surfaces separately if possible

## Moist Chamber Technique (Primary Observation Method)

See Appendix I in source text. Suitable for revealing sporulating structures of many taxa that would be missed in direct examination.

**For microfungi on plant debris:**
1. Cut pieces of substratum into uniform sections (circles or squares of designated size from broad-leaf/bark; given lengths of petiole/needle/stem)
2. Place on damp filter paper, paper towel, moistened sterile vermiculite, or moistened sterile sand in closed container
3. Inspect frequently for newly emerging fungal species; maintain correct moisture levels throughout
4. Continue observations for extended periods — many saprobes appear only in succession; sealing cut branch ends slows desiccation and recovers more endophyte-related species

**Alternative for bark-attached material:** Attach microfungi from leaf surfaces to microscope slides with double-sided adhesive tape to facilitate repeated observation and make voucher slides.

**Key trade-off:** Moist chambers underrepresent taxa compared to culture methods (Polishook et al. 1996 found at least 79% of direct-observation species also recovered by particle filtration + culture), but conditions in moist chambers may be suboptimal for some taxa. Moist chambers favor identifiable sporulating material but may suppress slow-growing taxa through competition.

## Culture Techniques

### Bills & Polishook Method (Most Effective — 1994)

Best for litter and decaying plant material:

1. Mix plant material with sterile water; pulverize in a sterilized blender
2. Wash resulting slurry through a series of mesh filters using distilled water
3. Collect particles **100–200 µm in diameter** (particle size critical — too large: multiple colonies per fragment obscure each other; too small: fungal cells disrupted)
4. Suspend particles in small volume of distilled water; wash again
5. Pipette suspension onto agar plates of two selective media:
   - **Malt agar amended with cyclosporin A** (inhibits rapid hyphal extension without blocking spore germination)
   - **Dichloran-rose bengal agar (DRBC)** (restricts colony diameter)
6. Subculture new emerging colonies onto range of standard media
7. Encourage sporulation: culture on cornmeal agar with pieces of sterilized banana leaves

**For well-decayed litter:** Culture techniques preferred over direct observation because small pieces of substratum are difficult to manipulate for microscopy.

### Alternative tissue-cutting method (Wilson & Carroll 1994):

- Cut plant tissue with sterile scalpel into small, equal-size pieces; plate fragments onto agar
- Record spatial order of plated fragments; reference fungi to each fragment
- Allows spatial relationship investigation; more labor-intensive than blender method; better for less-decayed material

### General guidelines:

- **Initial isolation medium:** Tap water agar (TWA) — effective and often stimulates sporulation; low-nutrient
- **General isolation:** Low-strength cornmeal-dextrose agar or potato-carrot agar
- **Temperature:** Moderate; approximating collection site conditions; lower temperatures reduce weedy mold overgrowth
- **Antibiotics:** Use cautiously — some microfungi are antibiotic-sensitive; not recommended universally; penicillin/streptomycin combination at low concentrations where bacterial contamination is severe
- **Nutrient-poor media** generally preferred (soil extract agar, carboxymethylcellulose medium CMC) — restrict hyphal extension; transparent (facilitates observation of minute colonies); inexpensive

### Cyclosporin-containing media:

- Permit germination of wide range of propagules but strongly restrict subsequent hyphal extension
- Not strictly selective but allow easy separation of colonies from high-density material
- Useful for slow-growing/rare taxa that would otherwise be overwhelmed

### Spore transfer to culture:

- Use sterile needle or micromanipulator (for small fungi with sparse samples)
- Some pyrenomycetes with forcible discharge: attach fruiting bodies to Petri dish lids so ascospores eject onto media surface
- Hyphal fragments can be transferred with fine sterile needle

## Observation and Isolation from Washed Litters

To exclude dormant propagules that are merely on (not nutritionally associated with) the substratum:

1. Wash needles/plant material in detergent; rinse in distilled water using shaker (Tokumasu et al. 1997)
2. After washing: either place in moist chambers for direct observation, OR culture

This technique distinguishes resident microfungi from aerial deposited propagules.

## Complete Inventory Protocols

### Two complementary approaches:

**1. Host-species-based sampling:**
- Sample fungi associated with each plant species; separate populations at different within-site locations
- Take into account physical variation in community structure (soil type, aspect, elevation)
- Sample several plant parts simultaneously: leaves (fallen, buried, attached, damaged); twigs/branches/bark; roots; flowers; fruits/seeds
- Sample fungi of living tissue (endophytes, biotrophs) at the same time
- Use geographic information system (GIS) for rare/targeted host locations

**2. Geographic/transect-based sampling:**
- For heavily decayed, unrecognizable plant material where host identity is lost
- Transects crossing valleys, streams, rocky outcrops, other physiographic features
- Samples of rotten wood and bark at specified points along transects
- Include some asymptomatic-appearing specimens for laboratory moist chamber study
- Sample at intervals during active growth seasons AND once during dormant periods

### Sampling Frequency

- Determine climatic regime first; identify dormant periods (hot/cold/dry spells)
- Sample less frequently (not suspended) during dormant periods
- Moist chambers allow collections at 2-month intervals to be observed continuously in lab — reduces field trips and site disturbance
- Record seasonal patterns of sporulation carefully

### Site Analysis (Critical Prerequisite)

- Map physical features: elevation, aspect, rock type, soil type, water content
- Measure temperature, precipitation, insolation (ideally over multiple years)
- Map vegetation (plant species identities, distributions)
- Note special features: rivers, caves, rock outcrops (distinct microclimates)

## Sampling Dead Woody Plant Material

**Choice of host species:**
- Woody plants preferred: wider range of microhabitats; longer life span; extended decay promotes species succession
- Consider: ease of identification in living and decayed states; ecological dominance; conservation status

**Life-form analysis before sampling:**
Document all available substrata types per plant:
- **Leaves:** fallen/buried/attached/suspended/laminae lesions; petioles; upper vs. lower surfaces; leaf hairs/spines/glands
- **Stems/branches/trunks:** fallen/buried/attached/suspended; bark surfaces; decorticated sections; cut/fractured surfaces; animal-damaged areas; bark fissures; spines
- **Roots:** cortex; exposed vs. soil-covered; damaged/decaying sections
- **Flowers/fruits/seeds:** fallen/attached; damaged/decaying; spines/wings; unfertilized ovules

**Temporal/successional considerations:**
- Include substrata from trees representing range of ages at death and decay stages
- If possible, determine local decay rates before sampling begins
- Long-term moist chamber observation is the practical alternative to tracking natural decay in situ

**Laboratory processing of wood samples:**
1. Examine with dissecting microscope as soon as practical after returning from field
2. Remove apparently sporulating material with scalpel or wetted needle; mount in water and lactofuchsin on glass slides
3. Examine with compound microscope (oil immersion; ideally Nomarski/DIC)
4. If identifiable sporulating structures present: proceed with identification; prepare multiple voucher collections + permanent mounts
5. Cultures: transfer spores or hyphal fragments to TWA with sterilized pieces of substratum

## Handling Non-Sporulating Cultures

Large proportions of isolates may fail to sporulate in culture:

- Aggregate into "non-sporulating" category for diversity analyses
- Compare colonies by growth rate, colony morphology, pigmentation, hyphal structure
- Molecular techniques (RAPD, rDNA sequencing) confirm taxonomic integrity of groups
- Create detailed database of character expressions to facilitate comparison

---

## Voucher Archiving

- Air-dry most microfungi; freeze-dry large/fragile specimens
- Provide physical protection (rigid containers) for large hyphomycetes, long-necked ascomycetes
- Fumigate or freeze to kill mites/beetles before long-term storage
- Note precise position of fruiting bodies on specimen; apparent interactions with other organisms
- Photograph both whole specimens and relevant microscopic details
- Line drawings (camera lucida) valuable for type specimens
- Database: specimen records (location, host, field/lab notes, identification) + species accounts

---

## Estimated Diversity at Survey Sites

Based on the Slapton Ley, Esher Common, and Mickleham Down UK study sites (Cannon & Sutton 2004): a well-sampled small UK site yields 2,500–3,000+ fungal species (most microfungi); only ~30% shared between two sites of comparable diversity; inventories far from complete even after extensive work. Extrapolation to 50,000-ha areas is difficult due to inadequate distributional data. Fungal:plant species ratio often approximates 6:1 at well-sampled temperate sites.
