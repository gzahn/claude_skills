# Fungal Parasites and Predators of Rotifers, Nematodes, and Other Invertebrates

> Source: Foster, Bills & Mueller (2004) Ch. 19 (Fungal Parasites and Predators of Rotifers, Nematodes, and Other Invertebrates — Barron).

---

## Overview

>300 recognized species of nematode- and rotifer-destroying fungi across Ascomycota, Basidiomycota, Chytridiomycota, Zygomycota, and Oomycota.

**Three functional modes:**
1. **Predators:** Extensive mycelial network in substratum; trapping devices (adhesive knobs/nets, constricting or nonconstricting rings) capture and penetrate prey; produce noninfective conidia externally
2. **Endoparasites of adults:** Minimal external mycelium; produce infective conidia or zoospores that adhere to host surface or are ingested; each spore attacks only one animal; hyphae proliferate and digest host from within; reemerge to produce new infective conidia
3. **Egg and cyst parasites:** (Not covered in this chapter — see Carris & Glawe 1989 for introduction)

## Obtaining and Culturing Bdelloid Rotifers (for Use as Bait)

Bdelloid rotifers are the primary bait animal for recovering rotifer parasites. They are ubiquitous in soil, manure, rotting wood, decaying leaves, compost, mosses/lichens, and freshwater/brackish/marine habitats.

### Establishing a Rotifer Culture from Soil

1. Sprinkle 1 g soil or debris on water agar (2%) plate; add 1–2 ml water; swirl gently; incubate 5–7 days
2. Locate bdelloid rotifers under dissecting microscope or low power of compound microscope
3. Aspirate a single rotifer with Pasteur pipette; deposit in drop of water on fresh water-agar plate
4. Irrigate with several drops of sterile water to separate from debris and contaminating microfauna; repeat several times
5. Place washed rotifer on water agar plate with drop of liquid 2% malt-extract agar OR 0% Czapek's agar (Czapek's agar without sucrose) + enough sterile water to produce thin film (1–3 mm) over surface
6. Incubate at room temperature (20–25°C)
7. If bacteria build up: add 5–10 ml sterile distilled water; mix thoroughly; draw off excess with Pasteur pipette; repeat until water is clear. Enough nutrient energy in 0% Cz to support bacterial growth for a large rotifer population
8. **Multiple single-rotifer cultures**: Start several simultaneously; discard any contaminated by flagellates, ciliates, or amoebae (they compete with rotifers and can consume parasite spores)
9. Population increases after several weeks; divide among plates monthly
10. **Maintenance:** Weekly transfers to fresh 0% Cz plates. Stock cultures for long-term storage: pour rotifers onto sterile soil in small culture bottles; allow to grow 1–2 weeks; then slowly dry; store dry at room temperature or refrigerated for years

### Long-Term Storage

- Bdelloid rotifers in dry sterile soil can be stored for years at room temperature or refrigerated

## Baiting Technique (Primary Method for Rotifer Parasites)

1. Culture rotifers to ~100–10,000 individuals on a water agar plate
2. Sprinkle 0.1–1.0 g soil or organic debris over the plate; swirl gently (leave clear areas for future microscopy)
3. **Alternative:** Make slurry (homogenize sample in water); add a few drops to bait plates
4. Incubate at room temperature; examine daily under low power compound microscope for parasitized rotifers
5. When infected (rotifers become immobilized and encyst): parasites complete cycle within 24–72 hours; sporulation on external hyphae begins at ~72 hours
6. If transmission occurs: numerous additional rotifers become infected within a week → epidemic

**Working with rare parasites:** Small samples required (common parasites obscure rare species). Use single-rotifer sources to seek host-specific or rare parasites. Different rotifer species as bait will increase species recovery.

## Isolating Parasitized Rotifers

1. Recover parasitized rotifer from plate with Pasteur pipette; deposit 1–several drops onto water agar
2. Locate rotifer under dissecting microscope
3. Pick up on needle with flattened tip; transfer to drop of clean water; irrigate; transfer to fresh water drop
4. Place on water-agar plate → conidia produced within 1–2 days
5. Add fresh rotifers to initiate epidemic

**For routine identification:** Pick up infected rotifers with fine needle; mount in acetic alcohol followed by lactophenol; examine and tentatively identify; save mounts as permanent records.

## Culturing Rotifer Parasites (Pure Culture)

1. Transfer infected rotifer to clean agar using fine needle or micromanipulator
2. Drag/push rotifer over or under agar surface to clean off debris and contaminating organisms
3. Transfer to plate with fresh **potato-dextrose agar + streptomycin sulfate (50–100 mg/L)**; incubate at room temperature
4. Within a few days, hyphae from rotifer grow into surrounding agar

**Alternative (hanging drop culture; Glockling & Shimazu 1997):**
1. Place drop of Grace's medium (modified with fetal bovine serum + streptomycin + penicillin) on sterile coverslip
2. Lift infected host into the drop with fine needle/micromanipulator
3. Invert coverslip over sterile cavity slide (drop hangs down)
4. Incubate in humid chamber made from Petri plate
5. Monitor daily with inverted microscope or low-power objective
6. Once mycelium visible: transfer to fresh cornmeal agar or other medium

**Inducing sporulation on nutrient-rich agar:** Place agar block with mycelium from pure culture onto water agar supplemented with rotifer extract or debris from old rotifer cultures (filter-sterilized through 0.2-µm Millipore filter). After sporulation begins, flood plate with fresh rotifer culture for massive simultaneous infections.

## Obtaining Nematodes (Baermann Funnel Technique)

**Build Baermann funnel apparatus:**
1. Cut bottom from 15-cm diameter aluminum foil pie plate
2. Glue nylon mesh to cover the hole
3. Rest lip of pie plate on edge of 15-cm glass funnel supported on ring stand
4. Connect base of funnel to 5-ml collecting tube with rubber tubing
5. Place 50–250 g soil/organic debris on double layer of facial tissue covering the mesh
6. Add water to funnel until it touches bottom of mesh
7. Cover with watch glass or inverted pie plate to prevent dehydration

**Operation:**
- Nematodes wriggle through tissue and mesh, sink by gravity to collecting tube
- After 6–12 hours: clamp rubber tube; remove collecting tube; carefully remove ~4 ml supernatant with Pasteur pipette; shake remaining liquid to disperse nematodes
- Pour onto surface of water-agar plate; inspect daily for up to 10 days for parasitized nematodes
- **Process samples frequently** (every few hours for large samples) — nematodes become inactive quickly if left overnight and may die before harvest

## Recovery of Nematode Endoparasites

**Two agar plate conditions for different parasite groups:**
1. **Freshly poured water-agar plates:** Maintains water film on surface; favors zoosporic endoparasites (*Myzocytium*)
2. **7-day-old water-agar plates:** Absorbs water quickly; favors hyphomycetous endoparasites (*Harposporium*, *Verticillium*)

**Processing:**
- Inspect plates for parasitized nematodes within first 48 hours (critical for rare/new species)
- Parasitized nematodes with developed conidiophores often visible within 48 hours
- Semipermanent lactophenol mounts of infected nematodes can be made for reference from original plates

**Host specificity note:** Many nematode parasites are host-specific; different nematode species in the funnel extract may harbor different parasites that will not transmit to other species — examine carefully.

---

## Culturing Nematode Endoparasites

Same methods as for rotifer parasites (PDA + streptomycin; or hanging drop culture).

**Washing nematodes before plating:** Low-speed centrifugation several times to control bacteria.

---

## Culturing Nematodes (for Predator Studies)

1. Some rhabditoid nematodes adapt and multiply on water agar plates
2. After 1–2 weeks: single-species population remains → use as nematode stock culture
3. **Growth media:** Water agar + 0.5–1.0 g oatmeal, cooked potato, soup powder, or peanut butter + few drops of nematode suspension; incubate at room temperature
4. **Pure cultures:** Collect a gravid female; wash; transfer by itself to fresh plate

## Recovery of Predatory Fungi

**Drechsler's technique (original method):** Pinch of leaf mold, rotting plant tissue, or organic debris added to Petri dish of dilute cornmeal agar previously inoculated with *Pythium* or *Phytophthora* → natural chain of events produces large populations of bacteria, nematodes, mites, amoebae → nematodes attacked by predatory fungi. The original *Pythium/Phytophthora* culture drives the initial ecosystem.

**Alternative:** Transfer debris from ditches, ponds, other aquatic habitats to sterile pond water in stackable, nested culture dishes; incubate; examine periodically for predators.

**Challenge nematode-predating macrofungi:** Grow isolate (*Pleurotus*, *Hyphoderma*) on water agar; add few drops of nematode culture; observe for capture events.

## Documenting New or Rare Parasites

Minimal protocol for describing new/rare parasites:
1. Prepare 10 semipermanent slide mounts of infected animals (fix in acetic alcohol; mount in lactophenol)
2. Obtain pure culture on PDA + streptomycin if possible; deposit in a public culture collection
3. Using washed animals on which conidiophores are developing: place in plate with bait population to initiate epidemic → if epidemic occurs, fix entire parasitized population in acidified alcohol; add equal lactophenol; slide + sealed coverslip = type material or voucher
4. Record distinctive features of fungus and its culture as diagrams, measurements, and photomicrographs throughout initial study
5. Verify Koch's postulates: (1) fungus constantly associated with disease; (2) isolated and grown in pure culture; (3) causes original disease when healthy organism inoculated; (4) reisolated from experimentally infected animal
