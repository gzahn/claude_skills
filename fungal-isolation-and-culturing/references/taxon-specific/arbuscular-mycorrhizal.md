# Mutualistic Arbuscular Endomycorrhizal Fungi (AM Fungi / Glomales)

> Source: Foster, Bills & Mueller (2004) Ch. 15 (Morton, Koske, Stürmer & Bentivenga).
> Spore extraction protocol: Revised 2019 from Nancy Johnson's protocol, McKenny &
> Lindsey 1987, and Julie Wolf. Integrated verbatim with all practical notes preserved.

---

## Overview

Arbuscular mycorrhizal (AM) fungi (Order Glomales, Phylum Zygomycota; ~163 species in 7
genera) form the most widespread mycorrhizal association among land plants (~80% of plant
species). **Critical limitation: AM fungi cannot be cultured apart from their plant hosts.**
The plant provides carbon via an arbuscule–plasmalemma interface; the fungus enhances
phosphorus acquisition for the plant.

**Key taxa:** *Glomus* (91 spp.), *Acaulospora* (35 spp.), *Scutellospora* (28 spp.),
*Gigaspora* (5 spp.), *Entrophospora* (4 spp.), *Archaeospora* (2 spp.), *Paraglomus* (2 spp.)

**Spore size range:** 20–800 µm diameter (complex subcellular wall layers diagnostic for
species identification — requires differential interference contrast optics)

## Spore Extraction from Soil — Complete Wet-Sieve Protocol

> This is the full lab protocol for physically separating AM spores from soil. It is a
> two-stage process: (1) wet-sieve separation of spores from mineral particles, followed by
> (2) sucrose density gradient centrifugation to float spores away from remaining debris,
> then (3) vacuum filtration onto gridded filter paper. Every practical note below is
> field-tested and should be followed exactly — corners cut here lose spores permanently.

### Equipment

- Tube rack with numbered 50 mL centrifuge tubes
- One or two sets of 4 numbered glass 100 mL beakers
  *(2 sets if floaters/sinkers or 25 µm/90 or 45 µm fractions will be examined separately;
  otherwise one set)*
- One or two sets of 4 numbered plastic filter paper holders and lids
- Four numbered, gridded petri dishes (for picking the 250 µm fraction within a few days)
  OR 4 baby food jars cleaned of labels; if no jars, use 50 mL plastic centrifuge tubes
- **Sieves:** 250 µm and 25 µm required; additional sizes (45, 90, or 180 µm) for silty
  soils that clog, or for inoculum production that requires specific size fractions
- Plastic 5000 mL pitcher
- Two white washbasins filled with hot water and soap
- Squirt bottle of tap water (wash bottle)
- Funnel
- Six metal stirrers with paddle-shaped ends
- Armed aspiration flask, sintered glass filter holder, blue metal clamp, glass funnel
- Forceps
- Magnetic stir bars
- Centrifuge (swinging-bucket rotor preferred; must fit 4 × 50 mL tubes)

### Reagents

- **2M sucrose** — kept refrigerated until use
- 0.45 µm gridded filter paper (for final vacuum filtration step)

### Step-by-Step Protocol

**Critical organizational rule:** You can extract 2 or 4 samples at a time (centrifuge
fits 4 tubes; balance is required). Label and assemble all samples and equipment IN ORDER
before starting. Every step must be performed in numerical order so samples are not
switched and no sample sits in sucrose longer than necessary.

**1. Record and label.**
Record sample number and date on data sheet. Label corresponding 50 mL centrifuge tube
(or baby food jar) AND filter paper cassette with sample number and date. Label each jar
twice. Prepare 2–3 labels per sample (one for the 250 µm sieve fraction, one or more for
other fractions). Affix labels to the edge of the weigh boat for easy removal. Do not get
soil on the sticky backs.

**2. Homogenize soil.**
Thoroughly homogenize each soil sample by shaking and turning the bag.

**3. Weigh.**
Weigh 25 g of homogenized soil into one plastic weigh boat. Record exact weight. Repeat
steps 1–3 for all remaining samples before proceeding.

**4. Load pitcher and begin wet-sieving.**
Dump weigh boat contents of sample #1 into the plastic pitcher. Rinse the weigh boat into
the pitcher with squirt bottle. Turn on cold tap water at high pressure (not so high that
soil is blasted out of the pitcher) and spray/agitate the soil until pitcher is about 1/3
full.

**5. First decant — 5-second settle.**
Allow soil to settle for up to 5 seconds. Pour the water (now full of spores) off the
settled particles and through the sieve stack. Do not spill any. Keep settled large
particles in the pitcher and off the sieves.

> **Sieve stack order — critical:** Stack sieves with SMALLEST MESH ON BOTTOM, or spores
> will be washed down the drain:
>
>     !  250 µm  !
>     !   25 µm  !
>
> Place the stack on the side of the sink near the drain, resting on a rubber stopper at
> a slight angle so it can drain freely. The 250 µm sieve can be touched gently with
> fingers to remove adhering roots. **NEVER touch the mesh of the finer sieves on purpose.**
> They cost several hundred dollars each and the mesh is extremely delicate.

**6. Repeat decants × 5 total.**
Repeat the spray/agitation and pour for a total of 5 sprays/pours. Monitor the 25 µm
sieve — if it clogs, water will drain out between the 25 µm and overlying sieve and spores
will go down the drain.

> **If clogged:** Pick up the overlying sieve(s) and hold them over the clogged sieve so
> no spores are lost. Gently squirt the clogged sieve with the wash bottle to move material
> and restore drainage. This is difficult. If you will be extracting this soil type in
> future, use intermediate-sized sieves above the 25 µm sieve to reduce the silt load.

**7. Discard pitcher residue.**
After the 5th pour, squirt remaining particles in the pitcher into the soil bucket. Do not
wash soil particles down the drain (drain clogging).

**8. Rinse the 250 µm sieve.**
Using cold tap water from the hose/faucet at gentle pressure, rinse the material in the
250 µm sieve to allow small sporocarps and small spores to pass through to the 25 µm
sieve. Wash all material to the corner (the angle between sieve wall and mesh that faces
downward). While doing this, very gently rough up the material with your index finger —
not your fingernail — and chase material around the mesh with the water. **This must be
very gentle.** The aim is to move material toward the corner, not to damage the mesh or
lose spores over the edge.

**9. Collect the 250 µm fraction.**
Using the squirt bottle, rinse the 250 µm sievings into the petri dish or jar labeled for
this sample. **Use as little water as possible** — the more water used here, the more
water ends up on slides. Unless picking spores within ~12 hours, cover with lid and
refrigerate. Petri dishes spill easily — place on a tray before moving.

**10. Collect intermediate fractions (if used).**
If using an intermediate sieve to prevent clogging, rinse it down with very low-pressure
water, then pass all material into the 25 µm sieve — UNLESS you are separating by size,
in which case process these sievings separately. Be careful: if water pressure is too
high, spores will jump out of the sieve and go down the drain.

**11. Collect the 25 µm fraction into centrifuge tube.**
Rinse sieve walls and move all material to the corner. Using the squirt bottle and funnel,
rinse all material from the 25 µm sieve into centrifuge tube #1. Rinse the mesh into the
funnel several times using little water. Rinse the inside of the funnel into the tube. If
the water in the tube touches the funnel, the outside of the funnel must also be rinsed
into the tube. If too much water is used and the tube overfills, pour contents back into
the 25 µm sieve and repeat.

**12. Wash equipment between samples.**
Wash and rinse sieves, pitcher, funnel, and weigh boat. Sieves need high-pressure water
sprayed from both sides to be free of spores, roots, and soil particles. Wash with hot
soapy water between each sample.

**13. Repeat steps 4–12 for all remaining samples.**

---

### Centrifugation — Stage 1: Water Spin

**14. Balance and load.**
When all centrifuge tubes are filled with water + spores + dirt, balance tubes by gently
adding water until levels match. Place tubes IN ORDER in the centrifuge. Always place and
remove tubes in the same order to prevent sample switching.

**15. Centrifuge: 3 minutes at 2500 rpm.**
Verify that required rpm is reached within ~45 seconds of starting, every time you use the
centrifuge.

**16. Pour off supernatant carefully.**
Remove tubes without disturbing the pellet (it is not firm). Pour off supernatant slowly
and completely into separate beakers. There is often a layer of root fragments hovering
just above the pellet — pour this off as well even though it looks like the pellet. Spores
that sink in water will be in the pellet. The supernatant contains root fragments,
air-filled spores, some fresh spores, and debris. Discard supernatant into the dirt
bucket.

> Practice this pour a few times before doing real samples — the pellet is loose and the
> line between pellet and the floating root layer above it is not always obvious.

---

### Centrifugation — Stage 2: Sucrose Float

**17. Add 2M sucrose.**
Fill each tube to 30 mL with cold 2M sucrose. Gently stir each tube with a paddle stirrer
to fully expose the pellet to the sucrose.

> **Work quickly.** Spores should be in sucrose for as short a time as possible to
> minimize lysis and loss of spore contents. Have everything ready before adding sucrose.

**18. Centrifuge: 1.5 minutes at 2500 rpm.**
While waiting, ensure the 25 µm sieve is clean and at hand, squirt bottle is full, and
numbered beakers are in order.

**19. Pour sucrose supernatant onto 25 µm sieve.**
Hold sieve at a slight angle. Take tube #1 and pour supernatant onto the sieve — no need
to be gentle here, as sucrose pellets are firm even with the tube inverted. Allow sucrose
to drain ~5 seconds, then rinse thoroughly with a lot of tap water — first down the sieve
wall, then down through the mesh so material accumulates in the lower corner. Even if
nothing is visible, rinse thoroughly.

> **Rinse with at least 30 mL of water** at this step — the sucrose must be fully washed
> away or it will interfere with vacuum filtration and spore preservation.

**20. Collect into numbered beaker.**
Once well rinsed, squirt everything from the sieve corner into the corresponding numbered
beaker. Use plenty of water; use a funnel if necessary. Wash and rinse the sieve between
each sample.

**21. Repeat for all tubes in order.**

---

### Vacuum Filtration onto Filter Paper

**22. Assemble filtration apparatus.**
Fit vacuum with a gridded filter paper and filter apparatus. Set up the armed aspiration
flask with tube connected to the inlet side of the vacuum pump.

**23. Wet the sintered glass support.**
Insert sintered glass filter support; wet it with a squirt of tap water.

**24. Place 0.45 µm gridded filter.**
Place the gridded filter onto the wet support. Rinse the inside of the beaker with the
squirt bottle into the filter apparatus to capture any spores adhering to beaker walls.
Ensure the filter is flat and not wrinkled, and that it covers the entire sintered area of
the support.

**25. Clamp and filter.**
Place the glass funnel over the filter and support, hold firmly, and clamp with the blue
metal clamp. Turn on the vacuum and rinse the sides of the filter apparatus with a squirt
bottle stream as water is drawn out. Pour beaker contents into the glass funnel, keeping
the level below halfway up the funnel. Rinse the beaker 3× into the funnel. Rinse the
inside of the funnel (spores adhere strongly to glass).

> **Stop rinsing the sides once the water level in the filter apparatus has dropped below
> the curved ridge** — continuing after that point creates a circular or uneven spore
> pattern on the filter paper.

**26. Even the spore field.**
When all water has gone through, turn off the pump. Add a small amount of water (less than
0.5 cm height in funnel) to resuspend spores. Turn pump back on. This resuspension step
produces an even "lawn" of spores across the filter — skipping it results in an uneven
distribution that makes counting unreliable.

**27. Remove filter.**
When all water has gone, unclamp the glass funnel **with the pump still running** (drops
from the funnel edge will displace spores if you unclamp with pump off). Then turn pump
off. Remove filter with forceps; place in numbered filter holder and cover.

**28. Refrigerate.**
Store finished filter papers in the refrigerator.

---

### After Extraction

**Sieve cleanup:**
Rinse sieves thoroughly with hard-stream water. Wash all other equipment with hot soapy
water. Sieves require water sprayed from both sides to be fully clean.

**Storage:**
- Spores in gridded Petri dishes with water: short-term at 4°C, 5–7 days
- Filter papers (dried): medium-term storage; rewet 24 h before counting
- Pot culture storage: see Pot Cultures section below

**Counting extracted spores:**
- Low numbers: count directly under stereomicroscope in gridded Petri dish
- High numbers: agitate dish to distribute evenly; count spores from 30–40 randomly
  chosen grid cells; average and multiply by total number of grid cells
- Alternative: place spores on gridded filter paper (prevents movement; better for
  distinguishing similar morphotypes)

## Field Collection and Soil Sampling

### General Protocol (McKenney & Lindsey 1987 / INVAM Standard)

1. Visual inspection for epigeous sporocarps at collection site
2. Collect soil to 15–20 cm depth; ensure sample contains all available roots
3. Mix sample with water in blender for 30 seconds
4. Pass slurry through nested sieves: **500, 250, and 38 (or 45) µm**
5. Examine 500 and 250 µm sieve material for small sporocarps and large spores
6. Centrifuge 38 (or 45) µm material in **20% and 60% sucrose density gradients**
   (50 mL tubes; swinging-bucket rotor optimal)
7. Pour supernatant from each tube into 38 (or 45) µm sieve; wash 1–2 min;
   rinse with distilled water; place in glass Petri dish for storage

**Alternative:** Wash sievings through pre-wetted filter paper in Buchner funnel under
vacuum; store filter papers in Petri dishes at 4°C for 5–7 days, or dry and store longer;
rewet 24 h before counting.

**Custom sieves:** Cut 2.5 cm diameter PVC pipe into 2 cm sections with nylon mesh on one
end (Tetko, Inc. supplies various mesh sizes).

### Sampling Design

- **Monthly sampling over 1–2 years** is most desirable; quarterly is more practical
- Single-date samples often misrepresent community (species have distinct seasonal patterns)
- Low sporulation sites: also establish trap cultures to recover nonsporulating species
- Low-richness sites: collect large numbers of samples (species-area curves often plateau
  after 10–15 samples in productive habitats)

## Root Staining (Assessing Mycorrhizal Colonization)

**Clearing and staining protocol (Koske & Gemma 1989 — safer than phenol-trypan blue):**
1. Clear root cells of cytoplasm and acidify
2. Differentially stain fungal cell walls

**Staining options:**
- Trypan blue in lactic acid + phenol (classic; phenol hazardous)
- Trypan blue in lactic acid alone — safer equivalent
- Acid fuchsin (Kormanik et al. 1980)
- **Chlorazol black E** (Brundrett et al. 1984) — highest contrast among fungal structures

**Note:** A few plant species (*Allium*, *Zea mays*, *Hordeum vulgare*, *Triticum aestivum*)
develop yellow pigments in colonized root areas visible without staining.

**Reference:** Sylvia (1994) provides detailed procedures for clearing/staining and
estimating percentage mycorrhizal root length.

## Pot Cultures (Only Way to Culture AM Fungi)

AM fungi **must be cultured in greenhouse pots on host plants.** Pot cultures serve as
trap cultures (to bait sporulating and nonsporulating fungi from field roots) and as
experimental microcosms.

**Requirements:**
- Low phosphorus growth medium (high phosphorus inhibits mycorrhizal development)
- Temperature, soil pH, moisture approximating conditions to which fungi are adapted
- Optimum light conditions to maximize photosynthetic carbon allocation to roots

### Culture Contamination Prevention (Critical)

1. No field soils or other potted plants in culture rooms (primary source of insects)
2. Remove surface dust; disinfect all surfaces at regular intervals
3. Handle pots carefully; avoid touching contents
4. Watering methods that prevent splashing and water movement between pots
5. Immediate chemical control response plan for insects (fungal gnats, Collembola are
   especially destructive)
6. Monitor with sentinel pots (sterile soil + host plant) placed randomly on culture tables

### Choosing Host Plant

- **Sudangrass (*Sorghum sudanense*)** — INVAM standard; supports colonization of >1000
  isolates of 98 species (all genera)
- **Big bluestem (*Andropogon gerardii*)** — superior at some locations (U. Minnesota)
- **Bahiagrass (*Paspalum notatum*)** — effective in subtropical Florida
- C₄ grasses are generally most compatible and productive (robust root systems; efficient
  carbon partitioning)

### Growth Medium

- **Sand** — inert; requires daily fertilizer monitoring
- **Sand + low-phosphorus loamy soil (1:1 v/v)** — INVAM standard; adjusted to pH 6.2;
  requires fewer fertilizer applications
- **Sand + vermiculite** — also effective

### Pot Size

- Volumes <300 cm³: rarely induce sporulation of more than 1–2 species
- **1000–1500 cm³:** most diverse sporulation within 4–5 months
- Larger volumes: similar results but require longer culture periods

### Establishing Trap-Pot Cultures

1. Collect ≥250 cm³ field soil with abundant roots; chop roots into small fragments; mix
   1:1 (v/v) with coarse sterilized sand to fill pot of ≥600 cm³
2. Sow host seeds; grow minimum 4 months; apply fertilizer only when plants show N
   deficiency (yellow leaves) or P deficiency (red leaves/stems)
3. Harvest when shoots cease growth (30–40 days post-cessation = peak sporulation —
   photosynthetic carbon reallocates to spores at this time)
4. Take root samples for colonization estimates; extract spores to measure abundance
5. If colonization and spore abundance are high: stop watering; dry pots slowly in cooler,
   shaded area; store in bags at 4°C (temperate isolates) or room temperature (tropical
   isolates) for minimum 30 days before extracting spores (spore dormancy period)
6. If spore numbers are low: second propagation cycle — remove shoots with scalpel;
   reseed same pot; maintain 3–4 more months

### Culturing Species from Trap Cultures (INVAM Protocol)

1. Extract spores; group similar morphotypes (color, size, other macromorphology); mount
   ≥10 spores on slides to verify identical morphology (these serve as permanent vouchers)
2. Place 50–100 spores of each morphotype in watchglass; remove all extraneous material
   with pipette; store at 4°C ≥24 h; remove unhealthy or hyperparasitized spores
3. Place pot with 10–12 day-old host seedlings (sorghum preferred) in sterilized glass
   bowl with water; gently tease roots apart
4. Remove 3 seedlings; wash away remaining soil/sand with gentle water stream; allow roots
   to entwine
5. Pipette spores from microtiter well directly along entwined roots (excess water drips
   without dislodging spores)
6. Plant seedlings in 3.8 × 21 cm cone-tainers (Stuewe and Sons) with sterile growth medium
7. Grow ≥90 days; examine for mycorrhizae and sporulation

**For single-spore cultures:** Pipette single spore onto roots at a midpoint location.

## Spore Storage and Archives

**Living specimens:**
- Store pot cultures at 4°C (temperate isolates) or room temperature (tropical isolates)
  in sealed bags, preferably slightly moist
- **INVAM** (West Virginia) — oldest and largest collection: ~1100 accessions, >86 species
- **BEG** (Bank of European Glomales) — network of researcher-maintained cultures
  registered at one central location
- Regional: University of Western Australia (Lynette Abbott); Instituto de Ecología y
  Sistemática Havana (Ricardo Herrera); Taiwan Agricultural Research Institute (Chi Guang Wu)

**Preserved specimens:**
- Spores mounted on glass slides: permanent vouchers
- Filter paper with extracted spores (dried): medium-term storage
- Spores in water in plastic Petri dishes: short-term at 4°C, 5–7 days

## Identification

Requires dissecting microscope for macroscopic properties; compound microscope with
**differential interference contrast (DIC)** optics for subcellular wall layers (<1 µm
thick, often colorless).

Spore wall characteristics (layers, ornamentation, flexibility) are primary diagnostic
features. Host plant identity does not indicate fungal identity (AM fungi have very low
host specificity).

In high-organic/high-microbial-activity soils (especially tropics), most field spores are
too degraded or hyperparasitized for reliable identification — use pot cultures instead.

**Key reference:** INVAM website (invam.caf.wvu.edu) — descriptions, images, keys for all
known species.

## Cautions

1. **Cannot culture in absence of host.** All requests to "grow AM fungi on agar" will
   fail. Pot culture on host plant is the only method.
2. **Spore dormancy.** Allow 30 days minimum at 4°C storage before using extracted spores
   as inocula.
3. **Sucrose exposure time.** Keep spores in sucrose as briefly as possible — lysis of
   spore contents begins quickly. Have everything staged before adding sucrose.
4. **Sieve handling.** Fine sieves (25 µm and smaller) cost hundreds of dollars and have
   extremely delicate mesh. Never intentionally touch the mesh. High-pressure water from
   both sides is the only cleaning method.
5. **Clogged sieves lose spores.** Watch the 25 µm sieve during every pour. If water
   backs up between sieves, spores are going down the drain.
6. **Sucrose rinse volume.** Use at least 30 mL water to rinse the sucrose fraction off
   the sieve into the beaker — thorough rinsing at this step is essential.
7. **Contamination monitoring.** Maintain sentinel control pots; dust during harvest is
   the most persistent contamination source — carry out setup/harvest in a separate room;
   mist air when handling cultures.
8. **Intraradical sporulators.** Use of roots-only inoculum can enrich cultures with
   species that sporulate inside roots (*Glomus clarum*, *G. diaphanum*, *G. intraradices*),
   causing abnormal dominance.
