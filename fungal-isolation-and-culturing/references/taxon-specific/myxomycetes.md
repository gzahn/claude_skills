# Mycetozoans (Slime Molds) — Collection, Isolation, and Culturing

> Source: Foster, Bills & Mueller (2004) Ch. 25 (Mycetozoans — Spiegel, Stephenson, Keller, Moore & Cavender).

---

## Overview

Mycetozoans are terrestrial amoeboid protists that produce aerial spore-bearing structures. Despite their common classification as "fungi," they are amoeboid protists — predators of bacteria, yeasts, fungal spores and hyphae, algae, and other protists. Key groups:

- **Eumycetozoa** (monophyletic):
  - **Myxogastria (Myxomycetes):** plasmodial slime molds; ~800 described species; primarily saprophytic decomposers; macroscopic plasmodia; diverse fruiting body types (sporangia, aethalia, plasmodiocarps)
  - **Dictyostelia:** cellular slime molds; 71 species; soil-dwelling; characteristic aggregate and slug formation
  - **Protostelia:** protostelids; 37 described species + >50 undescribed; all produce microscopic fruiting bodies
- **Acrasids** (polyphyletic):
  - *Acrasis*, *Pocheina*, *Guttulinopsis* (Heterolobosea)
  - Copromyxids (*Copromyxa*, *Copromyxella*)
  - *Fonticula*

**Identification note:** Most species identified from fruiting body morphology alone. All myxomycetes produce spores that germinate as amoeboflagellates. Cellular slime molds (Dictyostelia, acrasids) require culture for confirmatory identification.

## General Equipment Requirements

- Compound microscope and stereomicroscope (required for all groups)
- Basic microbiological culture facilities
- Stock cultures of bacteria (*E. coli*) and yeasts (*Saccharomyces cerevisiae*) as food organisms

**Critical note on dung vs. soil species:** Most dictyostelids and acrasids found on dung probably migrate from soil; they are not strictly coprophilous.

## Collection by Microhabitat

### Dead Aerial Plant Parts (DAPP)

Includes: herbaceous stems, leaves, old inflorescences/infructescences on standing plants — brown and showing signs of fungal decay.

- Collect at any time of year from any habitat
- Store in paper bags (if to be kept >1 day, use paper not plastic; plastic promotes mold overgrowth)
- Each collection: single plant; unique identifier; label with location, habitat, microhabitat, date
- Fresh samples can be plated immediately or air-dried and stored >1 year

### Litter

Fallen, recognizable dead plant parts on ground floor. Sample by:
- Soaking and plating (same as DAPP)
- Introduce sterilized wheat straws (~5 cm) into litter and collect after various periods
- Along transects: collect samples at regular intervals, place in sterile plastic bags (paper bags for longer storage), return to lab for moist chambers

### Soil / Humus Layer

Primary microhabitat for dictyostelids and many myxomycetes. Protostelids scarce; some Copromyxella.

**Cavender Method (primary soil method):**
1. Collect soil from multiple sites in habitat; dilute and suspend a set mass of soil in set volume of sterile distilled water (make 2 dilutions)
2. Spread set volume of suspension evenly on plate of weak nutrient agar (HI or wMY; see Appendix II)
3. Overlay with turbid suspension of *E. coli* in water; let lids air-dry open for several hours to evaporate excess liquid
4. Incubate at ambient temperature (below 20°C for samples from cooler regions) for 3–4 days
5. Examine for clones of cellular slime molds; subculture easily; identify using Raper (1984), Hagiwara (1989), Cavender (1990)

**Subculturing dictyostelids:** Spread 1.5% nonnutrient agar plates with 12- to 24-h pregrown *E. coli*; touch spores from sorus with sterile glass needle; drag across agar surface to clean; later subculture on 0.1% lactose-peptone medium streaked with light *E. coli* suspension.

**Feest modification (for myxomycetes in soil):** Use *Saccharomyces cerevisiae* instead of *E. coli*; myxomycete plasmodia appear several days after plating but rarely fruit in this method.

**Spatial resolution — soda-straw technique (Eisenberg 1976):** Core soil with soda straws to obtain subsamples of known position; plate each by Cavender method; individual clones can be mapped to within a few mm.

**Active vs. dormant cells:** Freeze subset of soil sample to –20°C overnight (kills active amoebae, not cysts/spores); plate frozen vs. unfrozen aliquots; percentage of clones missing from frozen plates approximates fraction of cells that were active.

### Bark of Living Trees

Most species visible only under microscope in laboratory. Field observation of macroscopic species (e.g., Physarales in summer): best 24–72 h after rain at 20–35°C; mature fruiting bodies appear 3–7 days after rain.

**Species selection:** Tree species, bark surface topography, epiphytic lichen/moss cover all influence which species occur. In eastern North America, richest substrata: red cedar (*Juniperus virginiana*), American elm (*Ulmus americana*), apple, grape.

**Moist chamber technique for bark (Gilbert & Martin 1933):**
1. Line Petri dish (100 × 15 mm) with sterile filter paper or paper towel
2. Cover bottom with single non-overlapping layer of bark pieces
3. Add 10–15 ml sterile distilled water to wet bark thoroughly; let soak 10–12 hours
4. Carefully remove excess water (or add more if bark absorbed it all)
5. Begin observation after ~24 hours (to detect *Echinostelium* and other tiny species); continue daily for 7–10 days
6. After 7–10 days: if not overrun by filamentous fungi, allow bark to dry completely then rewet (stimulates Licea species and some phaneoplasmodial species with sclerotia)
7. Incubate at room temperature (23–25°C); 12h light/12h dark cycle

**Observation:** 50–100× stereomicroscope. Label dishes on side (not lid). Many corticolous species are more difficult to culture than species from other microhabitats — bark species appear even in the same species collected from other microhabitats.

**Inducing fruiting:** Partially submerge bark in standing water to stimulate protostelids and acrasids (L. Olive, C. Stoianovitch). Provide migrating plasmodia with sterile straw or filter paper to migrate onto.

### Dung

Collect dung samples less than a day old with trowel (or inverted plastic bag as a glove); place in plastic bag.

**Processing:**
- Moisten with water if dry; place on moistened paper towels in finger bowls; cover
- Examine under stereomicroscope after 1–2 days; continue for several weeks
- To delay overwhelming Zygomycete growth (which can obscure small mycetozoans): **freeze sample overnight** — surface frozen solid, center still slightly soft; Zygomycetes delayed 2–4 days; easier to see acrasids, protostelids, dictyostelids. NOT recommended for tropical dictyostelids (may be sensitive to freezing)

**What to find:**
- *Copromyxa*, *Guttulinopsis* spp.: within 2–3 days on fresh dung; small and inconspicuous
- Dictyostelids: appear within days; very common
- Myxomycetes: may take longer; some appear on dried, older material

**Culturing coprophilous forms:** Spore-touch method → weak nutrient agar + *E. coli*. Clean bacterial contamination (common in Acrasid sori) by letting amoebae migrate 4–5 times through fresh bacteria streaks.

### Rotting Wood

Many large myxomycetes and many protostelids; cellular slime molds rare. Collect macroscopic fruiting bodies directly in field. 

**For microscopic species (protostelids):** Place small pieces of wood in standing water in Petri dish or on weak nutrient agar plates in moist chambers; examine daily for up to 2 weeks.

## Standardized Straw Method (Moore & Spiegel 1995)

For quantitative sampling of protostelids and other microscopic mycetozoans in dead aerial plant parts and litter:

1. Cut sterilized wheat straws to ~5-cm lengths; hang from monofilament fishing line in aerial microhabitat, or pin into litter with stainless-steel straight pins (colored-head pins easier to find)
2. Collect straws after various periods; plate using standard DAPP technique
3. Observe 3–5 days after plating, then every 3 days for ≥2 weeks; ~100 plates per observer per day
4. Sterilized control straws (not placed in field) plated as contamination controls
5. Two straws per plate; 2–10 min per straw

## Spore-Touch Isolation Technique

For protostelids, acrasids, and small myxomycetes on DAPP and litter:

1. Locate sporulating organism microscopically
2. Retrieve spores with sterile needle; transfer to plate of fresh agar streaked with food organism (*E. coli* or yeast)
3. Some bacteria from isolation plate will transfer along with spores — drag spores across agar surface with micromanipulator needle to clean
4. Include multiple spores initially; single-cell isolation only after culture established
5. **Media:** wMY (weak malt extract-yeast extract) or HI (hay infusion) agar most useful for DAPP and litter species; some *Echinostelium* spp. grow well on soft agar ± plant infusion

## Quantitative Sampling for Macroscopic Myxomycetes

1. Establish 20 m × 50 m plot (0.1 ha) in temperate/boreal forest; 20 m × 20 m in tropical or grassland habitats
2. Mark corners with stake-wire flags (seasonal) or wooden stakes/PVC (multi-year)
3. Examine all substrata along 3–4 parallel transects ≤5 m apart per visit; work from one end to other
4. Visit throughout year for several years; expect to find ~50–75% of species in year 1; declining new records in subsequent years; stop when no new species added in a given year
5. Each continuous patch of fruiting bodies = one clone; patches separated by >0.2 mm (one microscope field at 10×) = different clones; mark first-observed location to avoid recounting

**Optimal fruiting conditions (temperate):** 24–72 hours after heavy rain; 20–35°C daytime temperatures.

## Specimen Storage

**Macroscopic myxomycete collections:**
- Allow to dry completely before storage to prevent filamentous fungi and invertebrate pests
- Glue substratum bearing fruiting bodies to inside of lid of standard cardboard collection box (National Fungus Collections BPI standard: 4.5 × 10.5 × 2.0 cm; 10.0 × 9.25 × 2.0 cm for larger)
- Label on outside of lid; treat to kill insects and mites; store in cool, dry place
- Permanent slides of fruiting bodies stored with dried specimens
- Control insect damage before long-term storage: freeze collection for several days before storing (older insecticide methods no longer recommended)

**Cultures:**
- Most myxomycete cultures are in Physarida, Stemonitida, Echinosteliida orders
- Available from ATCC and some researchers (J. Clark, University of Kentucky; E. F. Haskins, University of Washington)
- Dictyostelid cultures preserved by lyophilization; available from ATCC and Kenneth B. Raper Cellular Slime Mold Collection (University of Ohio, Athens; J. C. Cavender curator)
- Protostelids: University of Arkansas Mycetozoan Culture Collection (Fayetteville; F. W. Spiegel) — ~25 species

## Myxomycetes Fruiting Body Types (Reference)

- **Sporangium:** Small, sessile or stalked; restricted size (~1 mm); definite shape; most common type
- **Aethalium:** Large, sessile, massive; round or mound-shaped; from one plasmodium (e.g., *Lycogala*, *Fuligo*)
- **Pseudoaethalium:** Many sporangia packed together, appearing aethalium-like (e.g., *Dictydiaethalium*)
- **Plasmodiocarp:** Sessile, elongated, simple or branched; forms when plasmodium remains intact in main veins

**Plasmodium types:**
- **Phaneroplasmodium:** Large, visible; pronounced shuttle streaming; reticulate; feeds above substrate; common in Physarales
- **Aphanoplasmodium:** Delicate, nearly invisible transparent veins; erratic streaming; grows submerged or very wet; characteristic of Stemonitales
- **Protoplasmodium:** Microscopic, rounded; no veins; gives rise to single tiny sporangium (e.g., *Echinostelium*)
