# Sequestrate Fungi (Truffles / Hypogeous Fungi) — Collection, Isolation, and Culturing

> Source: Foster, Bills & Mueller (2004) Ch. 10 (Sequestrate Fungi — Castellano, Trappe & Luoma).

---

## Overview

Sequestrate fungi sequester their spore-bearing tissues, preventing spores from discharging into surroundings. Usually hypogeous (underground), enclosed within a persistent peridium. ~150 genera, ~1,200 valid species in 38 families (11 orders across Ascomycetes, Basidiomycetes, and Zygomycetes).

**Key ecological roles:** Primarily ectomycorrhizal (most Ascomycetes and Basidiomycetes); also arbuscular mycorrhizal (Glomales/Zygomycetes); a few saprobic. Essential links in old-growth forest food webs (trees → fungi → small mammals → owls).

**Spore dispersal:** Almost entirely by animals (mammals, birds, arthropods) — aromatic attractant compounds produced as spores mature. This dependence on animals limits geographic dispersal and contributes to high endemism.

**Distribution peak:** Subtropical to middle latitudes in ectomycorrhizal forests. Richness correlated with diversity of ectomycorrhizal host plants; moist forests richer than very wet or very dry.

---

## Why They Are Difficult to Study

- Most fruit hypogeously — no visual clues in forest soil with humus/litter layer
- In compacted soil (campgrounds, picnic grounds, roadsides, skid trails), expansion forces sporocarps to surface or creates soil humps
- **Fruiting varies by season and location**; generally mirrors epigeous relatives (spring Ascomycetes; autumn Basidiomycetes in Pacific Northwest)
- **Surveys must continue 3–5 years** minimum to detect infrequently fruiting and rare species

## Collection Methods

**Finding hypogeous sporocarps:**
- Look for animal digs (holes where mammals have excavated fungi) — colonies produce multiple sporocarps maturing at different times; rake within and around digs to ~same depth as holes
- In Europe: dogs trained to find commercially valuable truffles
- Forest soil: rely on animal digs, habitat knowledge, and chance
- European method (Pilobolus-based): place rabbit dung pellets on soil as Myxobacteria/general bait — not for truffles but works for associated organisms

**Survey logistics:**
- Crew of 6 trained personnel can survey ~10 ha/day
- Survey every 2–3 weeks during fruiting season(s)
- Minimum 3 years, preferably 5 years
- Lab time: 3–4 days per day of fieldwork

## Voucher Specimen Collection and Preparation

**Field permits:** Some species protected at local/state/federal level; may require scientific collecting permits. Some species (e.g., *Oxyporus nobilissimus* polypore) cannot be collected at all — photograph and record instead.

**What to record on labels:**
- Date; specific location (country, state, county, landmarks); collector; habitat; plant community; transient attributes (fresh colors, odors, color after exposure/handling at 10–20 min and again after 2–3h or next day, color after drying, exudation of latex, color change of cut surface)
- Whether specimens found on soil surface, emergent, or completely hypogeous

**Storage in field:** Waxed-paper sandwich bags under cool conditions. **NEVER** use plastic wrap or airtight containers (anaerobic conditions cause rapid deterioration).

**Cut fresh sporocarps** to promote drying and examine interior:
- Cut each sporocarp at least in half; large specimens (>3 cm) into vertical slabs ~5 mm thick
- Many species have hard/impermeable peridia that prevent proper drying
- Cross-section with razor blade can be rehydrated with water or KOH for sectioning
- Well-dried specimens much easier to work with; once deterioration begins it proceeds rapidly

**Drying:**
- **Preferred:** Food dryer at ~30°C; good air circulation critical; avoid cooking with heat alone
- **Field without electricity:** Thinly slice (±2 mm); pack closely in sealed airtight container with predried silica gel; no specimens touching each other; minimize air space; 1–2 days in adequate silica gel → transfer to conventional dryer as soon as possible
- **Emergency (campfire):** String with waxed dental floss on large needle; suspend over campfire; OR lightweight aluminum mesh screens over fire; thin slices spaced for air circulation

## Live Cultures

**Most sequestrate fungi grow poorly or not at all in culture** — particularly Ascomycetes, Zygomycetes, and those Basidiomycetes in Russulaceae and Cortinariaceae.

**What can be cultured:**
- Some *Rhizopogon* spp. grow reasonably well — easy to isolate
- Saprobic species probably more culturable than mycorrhizal species
- A few culture collections maintain significant sequestrate fungi: US Forest Service Pacific Northwest Research Station; C.S.I.R.O. Division of Forestry (Australia)

**Isolation protocol:**
- **Use young sporocarps** — most successful
- Follow general protocols in Molina & Palmer (1982), Smith et al. (1994), and Ch. 3 (preservation-storage.md)
- Obtain mature voucher specimens from same collection to confirm species identity later
- Identity of cultures not backed by herbarium vouchers should be regarded as questionable (misidentification common due to lack of keys for most taxa)

**Note:** Identity of any culture without voucher collections is questionable — misidentification is common due to the poor state of keys for most sequestrate fungal taxa.

## Survey and Inventory Methods

**Sporocarp biomass** is the most common index of species richness and relative importance:
- Use dispersed 4-m² plots; sample area should total 800–1,400 m² for adequate stand-level representation (Luoma et al. 1991)
- Sporocarp distribution is strongly clustered → systematic (not random) plot placement reduces overestimation of biomass
- **Species-area curve** rarely stabilizes within a single study — Hunt & Trappe (1987) still had not stabilized after 32 months and 1,536 m² total sample area

**Species frequency** (presence/absence in plots or stands) provides another measure of importance, complementary to biomass.

**Molecular ectomycorrhizal typing:** More sensitive than sporocarp surveys — 192 ectomycorrhizal morphological types found from only 0.45 m² of soil cores, versus 43 sequestrate species from 27,000 m² of sporocarp plots in same area (Luoma et al. 1996a; Eberhart et al. 1996). However, labor-intensive and requires molecular confirmation.

**Definitions:**
- **Inventory:** cataloging of individuals on hand (specimens in herbaria)
- **Survey:** determination/delineation of extent of individual species at one location
- **Monitoring:** observation of extant individuals over time

| Order | Notable genera | Trophic mode | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pezizales (Ascomycetes) | *Tuber*, *Terfezia*, *Tirmania*, *Balsamia* | Ecto- or saprobic | True truffles; *Terfezia/Tirmania* in deserts; wind/water dispersed |
| Boletales (Basidiomycetes) | *Rhizopogon*, *Truncocolumella*, *Melanogaster* | Ectomycorrhizal | Host-specific to Pinaceae |
| Agaricales (Basidiomycetes) | *Hymenogaster*, *Gymnomyces*, *Macowanites* | Ectomycorrhizal | Russulaceae relatives |
| Cortinariaceae | *Thaxterogaster*, *Hymenogaster* | Ectomycorrhizal | *Cortinarius* relatives |
| Endogonales (Zygomycetes) | *Endogone* | Ecto- or saprobic | Some bright yellow; bird-dispersed |
| Glomales (Zygomycetes) | Sequestrate *Glomus* | AM mycorrhizal | Associated with woody perennials |
