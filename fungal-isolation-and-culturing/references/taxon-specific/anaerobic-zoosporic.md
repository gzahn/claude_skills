# Anaerobic Zoosporic Fungi (Rumen Fungi / Neocallimasticales)

> Source: Foster, Bills & Mueller (2004) Ch. 22 (Anaerobic Zoosporic Fungi Associated with Animals — Wubah).

---

## Overview

Obligate anaerobic zoosporic fungi (Order Neocallimasticales, Family Neocallimasticaceae, Class Chytridiomycetes) are the only known obligatorily anaerobic fungi. They reside in the gastrointestinal (GI) tract of mammalian herbivores and play a critical role in fiber degradation — especially penetration of recalcitrant plant cell walls. Discovered by Orpin (1975).

**5 recognized genera, 13–17 species (depending on taxonomic treatment):**
| Genus | Zoospore | Thallus | Key features |
|---|---|---|---|
| *Neocallimastix* | Multiflagellate | Monocentric | Most studied genus; releases zoospores through apical pore |
| *Piromyces* | Uniflagellate | Monocentric | Filamentous rhizoids |
| *Caecomyces* | Uniflagellate | Monocentric | Vegetative cell + zoosporangium |
| *Orpinomyces* | Multiflagellate | Polycentric, mycelioid | Globose sporangia |
| *Anaeromyces* | Uniflagellate | Polycentric, mycelioid | Fusiform/ellipsoidal sporangia |

**Identification characters:** Number of flagella per zoospore; monocentric vs. polycentric thallus; filamentous rhizoids vs. vegetative cells; zoospore ultrastructure (requires TEM); fermentation products; carbohydrate metabolism.

## Rumen Environment

- pH: 5.8–6.8 (fluctuates with feeding; buffered by bicarbonate/phosphate in saliva)
- Temperature: 38–40°C
- Gas composition: ~62% CO₂, 30% CH₄, 7% N₂, 0.6% O₂, 0.2% H₂, 0.1% H₂S
- Redox potential: –250 to –450 mV (maintained anaerobic by facultative bacteria consuming ingested O₂)
- Population levels in cattle: 7.6 × 10⁸ thallus-forming units per gram in rumen/reticulum; 10⁴ in feces

**All anaerobic fungi exhibit mixed-acid fermentation:** end products are formate, acetate, lactate, ethanol, CO₂, and H₂ (traces of succinate). Cannot grow on pectin as sole carbon source. Grow on wide range of monosaccharides, disaccharides, and polysaccharides.

---

## Host Range

Found in all continents wherever herbivores occur. Known from:
- **Ruminants:** cattle, sheep, goats, camels, llamas, oryx, deer, reindeer, bison, and many wild ungulates
- **Non-ruminant foregut fermenters:** kangaroos, wallabies, wallaroos
- **Hindgut fermenters:** horses, zebras, rhinoceroses, elephants
- **Rodents:** mara (*Dolichotis patagonum*)

Only *Piromyces* and *Caecomyces* isolated from horses and elephants. Host specificity at genus/species level is suspected but not established.

**Non-animal occurrence:** *Piromyces*, *Neocallimastix*, and *Orpinomyces* isolated from anoxic mud layers at bottom of pond on a dairy farm (Wubah & Kim 1994, 1995) — may play a role in nutrient cycling in anoxic environments.

## Sample Collection

### Critical Requirement: Strict Anaerobic Handling

All collection and laboratory work must maintain anoxic conditions. Use anaerobic glove boxes for laboratory work. Transport samples in sealed thermos flasks filled with CO₂-sparged warm water.

### Sample Sources

**Saliva:**
- Fit animal with esophageal fistula attached to tube; collect fluid from esophagus while animal is neither eating nor ruminating
- Freshly collected saliva from drooling animals can also serve as source
- Enrich with antibiotics — fungal population may be low

**Rumen fluid (primary source):**
- Animals fitted permanently with cannula or fistula (5–20 cm internal diameter)
- Feed high-fiber diet prior to collection to stimulate fungal growth
- Insert tube (sheep/goats) or hand (cows) through fistula
- Strain digesta through 3 layers of cheesecloth into container sealed immediately
- Transport in thermos flask: pre-warm with water (50–60°C); bubble CO₂ through water for ~20 min; empty water; add sample; seal
- Isolate within **30 minutes** of collecting digesta to maximize survival

**Feces:**
- Collect from ground in grazing pastures or from zoo cage floor
- For controlled experiments: tie plastic bag to rear of animal to collect feces before ground contact
- Store at 4°C; isolates obtained from feces dry for up to 1 year (resting stages survive desiccation)

**Whole GI tract (post-slaughter):**
- Remove GI tract intact immediately after slaughter
- Tie off various sections with twine to reduce cross-contamination
- Remove digesta (20–400 g fresh weight) from each organ: rumen, omasum, abomasum, small intestine, cecum, large intestine; also collect pre-slaughter fecal sample
- Maintain samples at ~37°C between slaughter and fungal enumeration
- Determine pH of each organ's digesta with portable pH meter

## Isolation, Culture, and Maintenance

### Laboratory Setup

- **Strict anaerobic conditions required:** anaerobic glove box (commercial) equipped with incubator at 39°C
- Atmosphere: 95% CO₂ / 5% H₂; oxygen excluded by palladium catalyst; moisture controlled with desiccants
- Field conditions: use Hungate tubes with media only

### Isolation Methods

**Agar overlay method (Orpin 1975 — classic):**
1. Overlay partially molten agar with filtered rumen fluid
2. Incubate at 39°C for 48 hours — zoospores settle and form individual thalli from which pure cultures obtained

**Plate culture technique (Lowe et al. 1985):**
- Inoculate plates with filtered rumen fluid or dilutions thereof

**Roll-bottle method (Joblin 1981):**
- Inoculate dilution series of molten agar medium with filtered rumen fluid
- After incubation: individual colonies form axenic cultures (may not be bacteria-free unless antibiotics added)

**Antibiotics to suppress bacteria (essential for pure cultures):** Add to isolation media — penicillin G, streptomycin sulfate, neomycin, and chloramphenicol (various combinations, see Lowe et al. 1985; Orpin & Joblin 1988; Borneman et al. 1989; Wubah et al. 1991). Without antibiotics, contamination invariably occurs after 2–3 transfers.

**Most common contaminant:** Methanogenic bacteria (which have synergistic relationships with anaerobic fungi — see trophic relationships section).

### Culture Media

**Undefined medium (most commonly used):**
- Components: centrifuged rumen fluid + tryptone + yeast extract + carbon source + buffer + L-cysteine (reducing agent) + vitamins (Orpin & Joblin 1988; Wubah et al. 1991c)

**Semi-defined medium:**
- Same as above but omitting rumen fluid; some isolates grow well (Orpin & Greenwood 1986; Lowe et al. 1985)

**Defined medium (for some strains):**
- *N. frontalis* maintained without yeast extract and rumen fluid (Lowe et al. 1985)
- Basal Anaerobic Medium (BAM): volatile fatty acid mixture + hemin substituted for rumen fluid (Borneman et al. 1989; Wubah et al. 1991a,b,c,d)

**Carbon sources:** Cellulose, hemicellulose, xylan, barley straw, wheat straw, filter paper work well; also monosaccharides, disaccharides, polysaccharides.

### Maintenance

- **Transfer interval:** Every 3–4 days into fresh BAM (Wubah et al. 1991d)
- **Exception:** Some cultures maintained for months on plant tissues at 39°C without subculturing (Joblin 1981)
- **Long-term storage:** Cryopreservation at –70°C in liquid nitrogen with 5% DMSO or 10% (v/v) glycerol as cryoprotectant (Yarlett et al. 1986b; Phillips & Gordon 1988)

## Life Cycle

**Life cycle stages (best documented for *Neocallimastix* and *Caecomyces equi*):**
1. Motile-zoosporic stage alternating with vegetative-zoosporangial stage
2. Cycle duration: 24–32 hours in vivo and in vitro; zoosporogenesis can begin 8 hours after encystment under ideal conditions
3. **Resistant sporangial (RS) stage:** Dark brown melanized resting structure; forms ~36 hours after zoospore inoculation; provides survival outside host (enables transfer via feces); melanin pigment confirmed histochemically

**Transfer between hosts:** Via ingestion of feces (fecal-oral route); zoospores and melanized sporangia in both fresh and dry feces. Newborn lambs in limited contact still acquire rumen fungi (contact with mother not required — possibly airborne/environmental route).

## Enumeration

**Most Probable Number (MPN) method — endpoint dilution (Theodorou et al. 1990):**
1. Prepare 10-fold dilution series of sample in antibiotic-containing BAM + 10% clarified ruminal fluid
2. Each dilution (10⁻³ to 10⁻⁶): transfer 10-ml portions to 3 replicate enumeration tubes containing ~100 mg wheat straw
3. Incubate at 39°C; check daily with inverted microscope for zoospore motility + rhizoids/zoosporangia on plant fragments
4. Score positive: motile zoospores in culture supernatant AND rhizoids/zoosporangia on plant fragments
5. Most positive tubes scored within 3–4 days; confirm all negatives at day 10
6. Express as thallus-forming units (TFU) per gram dry matter

**Alternative methods:**
- Direct counts of individual zoospores and zoosporangia (Joblin 1981; Windham & Akin 1984) — in vitro and in vivo
- Colony-forming units per gram dry weight of feces (Breton et al. 1991)

## Ecological Context

**Role in fiber degradation:** Rhizoids penetrate plant tissue more effectively than bacteria or protozoa (access material unavailable to other rumen microbes). Penetrate lignified cell walls, cuticle (through stomata, cracks), and secondary wall layers. Weaken textural strength of forage residue by ~40% after 9 days.

**Synergism with methanogens:** Co-culture with methanogens increases cellulose degradation (53% in monoculture → 69–98% in tri-culture). Shifts fermentation from electron-sink products (lactate, H₂) to more reduced products (CO₂, CH₄, acetate). Methanogens attach to zoospore/thallus surfaces to utilize hydrogenosome products.

**Interaction with protozoa:** Protozoa suppress fungal zoospore populations; defaunated (protozoa-removed) animals show 2–4× increase in fungal zoospores and zoosporangia.
