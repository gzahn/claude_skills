# Endophytic Fungi — Isolation and Culturing

> Source: Foster, Bills & Mueller (2004) Ch. 12 (Endophytic Fungi — Stone et al.).

---

## Overview

Endophytic fungi are detected and quantified primarily by isolation from surface-sterilized host tissue. This is the most practical approach for diversity inventories, though obligate biotrophs may be undetected/underrepresented, and isolates failing to sporulate require characterization by other means.

**Critical design factors:** sampling unit size, surface sterilization procedure, culture medium composition, selective agents, incubation conditions. Optimize these for each new host/tissue type combination before initiating a full study.

---

## Sample Collection and Handling

- Process samples within 48 hours of collection
- Air-dry samples to remove surface moisture before transport/storage
- Use cotton, Tyvek, or paper collecting bags/envelopes — **not plastic bags** (condensation promotes surface mold growth)
- If plastic bags are necessary, leave them open for air circulation

**Tissue selection for maximum diversity:**
- Older foliage > younger foliage (accumulated more infections)
- Perennial > annual plants; evergreen > deciduous
- Sampling multiple host species in one area is more efficient than sampling one host species throughout its range
- Vary the host organs and tissues sampled (leaves, twigs, bark, roots, seeds, stems)

---

## Surface Sterilization Protocols

Surface sterilization removes epiphytic fungi from the tissue surface to reveal only internal (endophytic) colonists. The choice of sterilant and duration must be validated for each host species and tissue type.

**Standard surface sterilization sequence (most common approach):**
1. Brief ethanol dip (70–96%, 30 s to 1 min) — wetting agent
2. Oxidant treatment (NaOCl or H₂O₂) — primary sterilant
3. Rinse in sterile water or brief ethanol — remove residual sterilant

**Validated protocols from published studies (Table 12.3 in source):**

| Protocol | Host/tissue | Reference |
|---|---|---|
| EtOH 96% 1 min → NaOCl 2% available Cl 7 min → EtOH 96% 30 s | Conifer twigs | Petrini & Müller 1979 |
| EtOH 75–96% 1 min → NaOCl 2–4% available Cl 3–5 min | Conifer needles; Quercus leaves/twigs | Carroll & Carroll 1978 |
| EtOH 75–96% 30 s → rinse sterile water | Ulex twigs; Pinus/Fagus twigs; Acer/Betula/Picea roots | Petrini & Fisher 1988 |
| EtOH 96% 1 min → NaOCl 10% available Cl 5 min → EtOH 96% 30 s → sterile water | Crataegus, Salix, Vaccinium leaves; Festuca leaves/culms | Schulz et al. 1993 |
| EtOH 96% 1 min → NaOCl 3% available Cl 10 min → EtOH 70% 30 s | Sequoia leaves | Espinosa-Garcia & Langenheim 1990 |
| EtOH 99% 1 min → NaOCl 8.7% available Cl 5–120 min → EtOH 99% 30 s | Castanea shoots | Bissegger & Sieber 1994 |
| EtOH 95% 1 min → NaOCl 20% available Cl 3 min → EtOH 95% 30 s | Pteridium rhizomes, rachis, pinnules | Petrini et al. 1992a |
| EtOH 70% 1 min → H₂O₂ 15% 15 min → EtOH 70% 1 min → sterile water × 2 | Pinus needles | Hata & Futai 1995 |
| EtOH 99% 30 s only | Abies/Fagus/Picea/Pinus roots | Ahlich & Sieber 1996 |
| AgNO₃ 1%, then precipitate with 5% NaCl | Roots/stems of grasses (Gaeumannomyces graminis) | Cunningham 1981 |

**Sterilant notes:**
- **NaOCl (household bleach):** Most common; varies in concentration between brands — always specify percentage of available chlorine, not dilution. Works for most tissue types.
- **H₂O₂ (3–35%):** Effective alternative to bleach; lower residual toxicity
- **Ethanol (70–99%):** Primarily a wetting agent and mild disinfectant; do NOT use as sole sterilant
- **Formalin (30–50%):** Effective but highly toxic; rarely used now
- **Mercuric chloride (0.01–0.1%):** Very effective but toxic and hazardous; rarely used now; may be necessary for some roots (Summerbell 1989) and Eucalyptus leaves
- **Peracetic acid (0.35%):** Used for Alnus stems (Fisher & Petrini 1990)

**For root tissues:** Serial washing (Harley & Waid 1955) is often preferable to surface sterilization — better at recovering representative frequencies of root colonists. Use large vessel for vigorous agitation. Ultrasonic cleaning apparatus removes surface contamination most completely (Holdenreider & Sieber 1992).

**Validation controls:** Include surface-wipe control plates (swab tissue surface before sterilization onto agar plate) to verify sterilization effectiveness. Include uninoculated media plates to check for contamination.

## Tissue Processing

**Cutting to segments (standard):**
- Cut tissue into small segments; place on agar surface in serial order so positional/distribution effects can be determined
- Multiwell plates instead of Petri dishes can prevent cross-contamination of segments from fast-growing fungi
- Speed up cutting with: glass microscope slide as straightedge guide + razor blade; pasta cutter for thin leaf strips

**Maceration + particle filtration (for diversity estimates):**
1. Surface wash and disinfect tissue
2. Macerate in laboratory blender ("Stomacher Blender" works well)
3. Filter through wire mesh prescreen
4. Force fine particles through polypropylene mesh filters with sterile distilled water; use 105–210 µm size particles (one colony or none per particle)
5. Resuspend trapped particles in water or 0.2% agar; plate on standard isolation media
6. Check plates daily — isolate new colonies before overgrowth by fast-growing species

**Grass seeds (Neotyphodium/Clavicipitaceous endophytes):**
1. Free seeds from glumes by rubbing vigorously between hands (30–40 seeds)
2. Place in 250-ml beaker; cover with **50% bleach solution**; agitate 15–20 minutes
3. Decant bleach; replace with 100 ml sterile distilled water; agitate 5 minutes
4. Remove with sterile forceps; press into PDA
5. Use ~20 plates with 3 seeds per plate; seal with Parafilm
6. **Discard rapidly growing fungi appearing in first 2 weeks** — these are contaminants, not the slow-growing Neotyphodium

## Culture Media

**Standard primary isolation media:**
- MEA (1–2%) ± yeast extract (0.1–0.2%) — most commonly used
- Starch-milk agar (see media-recipes.md) — especially useful for endophytes
- Water agar — reduces contamination but produces diffuse, hard-to-recognize colonies

**Best combination (Bills & Polishook 1992):** 1% malt extract + 0.2% yeast extract + 50 ppm streptomycin + 50 ppm chlortetracycline — gave highest species richness for Chamaecyparis thyoides twigs and leaves.

**Incubation:** 18–25°C (reflect natural host conditions); incubation temperature and light cycles influence sporulation. Seal plates with Parafilm to prevent desiccation (but note: slow desiccation often promotes sporulation of coelomycetes — trade-off). Use multiwell plates or humidity-controlled growth chambers to balance desiccation vs. sporulation.

| Agent | Activity against | Concentration | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cyclosporin A | General filamentous fungi (growth inhibition) | 2–10 mg/L | Allows slower-growing fungi to develop |
| Amphotericin B | Filamentous fungi | 0.5–10 mg/L | Sterol synthesis inhibitor |
| Dichloran (Botran) | Mucorales, Penicillium | 2–100 mg/L | Less hazardous substitute for PCNB |
| PCNB | Aspergillus, filamentous fungi | 100 mg–1.0 g/L | Carcinogen — use dichloran instead when possible |
| Ampicillin | Bacteria | 100–300 mg/L | |
| Lactic acid | General suppression | Variable | Organic acid selective agent |
| Tannic acid | General suppression | Variable | Organic acid selective agent |

**Subculturing:** Move fungi from selective media to media without inhibitors as quickly as possible to allow normal sporulation for better identification.

## Key Ecological Groups of Endophytes

### Clavicipitaceous Grass Endophytes (Neotyphodium, Epichloë, Balansia)

- Systemic infections — colonize virtually all plant tissues
- **Neotyphodium** (asymptomatic, seed-borne in cool-season grasses): white, cotton/felt-like colonies on PDA at room temp; slow-growing; conidia reniform to subulate
- **"P-endophytes"** (Gliocladium-like, Phialophora-like): on starch-milk agar produce bright-yellow pigment (not in Clavicipitaceae); can be induced to sporulate by storing cultures at 4–5°C for 6–10 weeks
- **Epichloë**: conidial stromata on host inflorescences; white then yellow-orange as perithecia develop
- **Balansia**: conidial stromata on culms at nodes or on leaves; white, purple, or brown

### Xylariaceous Endophytes (Xylaria, Hypoxylon, Nemania)

- Ubiquitous in virtually all temperate woody hosts
- Usually produce anamorphic states in culture; anamorph important for identification
- Cultures of Hypoxylon have been obtained from internal stromatal tissue of Xylaria (isolation risk: contaminate rather than intended fungus)

### Nonclavicipitaceous Endophytes (Leotiales, Rhytismatales, etc.)

- Dominant group in conifer needles (Rhabdocline, Phyllosticta, Lophodermium) and many other hosts
- Limited infection domains (no more than a few cells typically)
- Identification requires matching to sporulating state on host

## General Sampling Strategy

- **Smaller sampling units** → greater recovery of diverse species/genotypes; **larger units** → miss rare species, recover mixed genotypes
- **Older foliage** → greater species diversity than younger foliage
- Sampling many host species in one area > intensively sampling one host throughout its range
- **To maximize diversity:** intensive selective sampling of limited tissue from individuals on ecologically varied sites; vary culture conditions, segment size, and medium composition
- **A single host often harbors 1–several species unique to it** — include multiple hosts in any diversity study

---

## Quantifying Endophyte Infection Frequency

Standard metrics:
- **Infection frequency:** number of tissue segments positive for a species / total segments plated
- **Species richness:** total number of species per tissue type / per host / per site
- **Relative importance:** frequency of one species / sum of frequencies of all species

**Caution on large sample units:** When segment size is large relative to infection domain (which is often microscopic — as small as a few cells), a single plate can contain multiple independent infections of the same species, or miss rare species entirely. Microdissection reveals far more heterogeneity than standard segment plating. Results should be interpreted accordingly.
