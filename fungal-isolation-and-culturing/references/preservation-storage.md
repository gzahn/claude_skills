# Preservation and Storage of Fungal Cultures

> Source: Foster, Bills & Mueller (2004) *Biodiversity of Fungi: Inventory and Monitoring Methods*. Academic Press. Ch. 3: Preservation and Distribution of Fungal Cultures (Nakasone, Peterson & Jong).

---

## Overview of Methods

| Method | Duration | Cost | Equipment | Best for |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Serial transfer | <1 year | Low | None | Small collections; active strains |
| Oil overlay | Several years (up to 32 yr) | Low | None | Non-sporulating/mycelial; non-freezable |
| Distilled water immersion | 2–10 years | Very low | None | Basidiomycetes, oomycetes, ascomycetes |
| Organic substrata (wood, grain) | 2–10 years | Low | None | Wood fungi, Sclerotinia, Rhizoctonia |
| Silica gel / glass beads | 4–11 years | Low | Desiccator | Sporulating species; can't lyophilize/cryo |
| Soil/sand | Variable | Very low | None | Rhizoctonia, Septoria, Pseudocercosporella |
| Freezing –20° to –80°C | 5–13 years | Medium | Mechanical freezer | Most fungi with glycerol/DMSO |
| Liquid nitrogen (–196°C) | Permanent | High | LN₂ tank | All fungi; preferred for non-sporulating |
| Lyophilization (freeze-drying) | Permanent (30+ yrs) | High | Lyophilizer | Sporulating species with ≤10 µm spores |
| Sclerotia/resting structures | 1–5 years | Low | None | Myxomycetes, Magnaporthe, Phymatotrichum |

**General principle:** Whenever possible, preserve with a permanent method (lyophilization or liquid nitrogen cryopreservation). These are reserved for strains with critical characteristics and type specimens. Non-permanent methods are appropriate for strains in constant use.

## Short-Term Preservation (<1 year)

### Serial Transfer (Continuous Growth)

**Protocol:**
1. Transfer inoculum from actively growing culture to test tubes (screw-cap or cotton/foam-plugged) or Petri dishes (wrapped with Parafilm)
2. Use appropriate agar medium; alternate nutrient-rich with nutrient-poor media at each transfer to maintain healthy cultures
3. Keep at room temperature or 4°C
4. Check periodically for contamination and desiccation

**Storage temperatures:**
- Room temperature or 4°C for most fungi
- Most filamentous fungi survive at least 1–2 years at 4°C
- Oomycetes, and some basidiomycetes (*Boletus*, *Coprinus*, *Cortinarius*, *Mycena*): transfer monthly if kept at 16°C

**Freezing for extended short-term storage:**
- Cultures on agar slants in screw-cap bottles or tubes can be placed directly in freezer at –20°C (Carmichael 1956)
- Or stored at –70°C to further increase interval between transfers

**Disadvantages:** Labor-intensive; risk of contamination; morphological/physiological changes with repeated transfers; sporulation or pathogenicity may be lost.

## Long-Term Preservation Methods

### Oil Overlay

**Best for:** Mycelial or non-sporulating cultures not amenable to freezing or freeze-drying; basidiomycetes; oomycetes.

**Protocol:**
1. Sterilize high-quality mineral oil or liquid paraffin by autoclaving at 15-lb (6.8-kg) pressure for 2 hours
2. Remove entrapped moisture: heat in drying oven at 170°C for 1–2 hours (optional)
3. Cover fungal cultures on agar slants with ~10 mm of oil — entire agar surface and culture must be completely submerged
4. Keep tubes upright at room temperature (15–20°C); for *Pythium* and *Phytophthora*: 12°C
5. Check oil level periodically; add more if necessary

**Duration:** Several years; exceptional cases up to 32 years.

**Retrieval:** Remove small amount of colony; drain as much oil as possible; place on appropriate medium. May need to subculture several times to obtain a vigorous oil-free culture.

**Advantage:** Also reduces mite infestations.

**Caution:** Fungi continue to grow (albeit slowly), so selection for mutants can occur over long storage.

---

### Immersion in Distilled Water

**Best for:** Basidiomycetes, oomycetes, ascomycetes, hyphomycetes, plant pathogenic fungi, ectomycorrhizal fungi, human pathogens/yeasts.

**Protocol:**
1. Use sterilized straws or Pasteur pipettes (large-diameter end) to cut disks from growing colony edge
2. Transfer disks to sterile cotton-plugged or screw-cap test tubes filled with several ml of sterile distilled water
3. **Alternative for sporulating fungi (McGinnis et al. 1974):**
   - Incubate agar slants at 25°C for several weeks to induce sporulation
   - Add 6–7 ml sterile distilled water aseptically; scrape surface gently with pipette to produce spore/mycelial slurry
   - Remove slurry with pipette; place in sterile 2-dram glass vial; tighten cap; store at 25°C
4. Loosely capped test tubes: store at room temperature
5. Tightly capped tubes/vials: store at 4°C

**Duration:** Most basidiomycetes survive 2 years at 5°C; viability decreases after 5–10 years. Ascomycetes and mitosporic forms survived up to 10 years at 20°C (Johnson & Martin 1992). *Warning: Do not store basidiomycetes at 20°C* — only 26% survival reported (Johnson & Martin 1992).

**Retrieval:** Remove disks aseptically; transfer to fresh agar medium.

### Organic Substrata

**Wood (for wood-inhabiting basidiomycetes and ascomycetes):**

1. Add small pieces of untreated beech wood (12 mm diam. × 6 mm thick; ~60 pieces/100 ml) to 2% malt-extract broth; sterilize 20 min at 121°C; sterilize again 24 hours later
2. Drain ~15 wood chips; place on colony growing on malt extract agar in Petri dish; seal with Parafilm; allow fungus to colonize (10–15 days)
3. Transfer inoculated chips to sterile test tubes (18 × 180 mm) with 6–7 ml of 2% malt agar; plug with cotton; incubate 1 week
4. Replace cotton with sterile Parafilm and aluminum foil; store at 4°C

**Duration:** Some wood-inhabiting basidiomycetes and ascomycetes: up to 10 years. **Warning:** If fungi do not vigorously colonize chips, the method fails.

**Cereal grains (Sclerotinia, Magnaporthe, Leptosphaeria, Rhizoctonia):**

1. Soak barley, oat, or wheat grains overnight in water with chloramphenicol (250 µg/ml)
2. Remove water; autoclave 1 hour at 121°C over 2 consecutive days; fill screw-cap vials
3. Inoculate from margin of actively growing culture; incubate 23–27°C for 7–10 days
4. Dry thoroughly in desiccation chamber; tighten caps; wrap with Parafilm; store at –25°C

**Duration:** Up to 10 years.

**Agar strips (vacuum-dried; Nuzum 1989):**
- Grow fungi on appropriate media in Petri dishes
- Cut 1-cm strips from colony edge; place in sterile Petri dishes; dry 1 week at room temperature
- Transfer to sterile ampoules; vacuum-dry; seal
- *Pythium*, *Rhizoctonia*, some basidiomycetes: survived 18 months; ascomycetes and mitosporic forms: 3–5 years

**Insect or plant tissue:** Maintain pathogenic fungi on host tissue; e.g., roots of plants infected with *Pyrenochaeta* and *Thielaviopsis* can be dried and frozen.

---

### Soil or Sand

**Best for:** *Rhizoctonia*, *Septoria*, *Pseudocercosporella*, and similar soil fungi.

**Protocol:**
1. Fill 60-ml glass bottles to two-thirds capacity with sand or loam soil (20% water content); sterilize by autoclaving 20 min at 120°C; sterilize again after cooling
2. Add 5 ml sterile distilled water to culture; scrape colony surface to produce spore/mycelial slurry
3. Add 1 ml slurry to each bottle; allow 2–14 days growth at room temperature; cap loosely; store at 4°C

**Retrieval:** Sprinkle a few grains of soil onto fresh agar medium.

---

### Silica Gel

**Best for:** Sporulating fungi; when lyophilization or liquid nitrogen are unavailable. *Not* suitable for *Pythium*, *Phytophthora*, and similar oomycetes.

**Duration:** 4–5 years (sporulating fungi); up to 11 years for dictyostelids.

**Protocol A (sporulating strains; Perkins 1962):**
1. Fill screw-cap tubes partially with 6- to 22-mesh silica gel without indicator dye (heat-sterilized 90 min at 180°C)
2. Suspend spores in 10% (v:v) dry powdered skim milk in distilled water, cooled to 4°C
3. Chill silica gel to 4°C; place in ice-water bath
4. Add spore suspension to wet ~¾ of gel (0.5 ml/4 g silica); leave in bath 30 minutes
5. Store with caps loose at room temperature for 1–2 weeks
6. Check viability (shake a few crystals onto agar); if viable, tighten caps; store in sealed container at 4°C

**Protocol B (nonsporulating strains; Perkins 1977):**
1. Use cotton-plugged test tubes (13 mm diam. × 100 mm) partially filled (65 mm) with 12- to 20-mesh silica gel; heat-sterilize at 180°C for 2 hours
2. Grow cultures 1 week on agar slants; scrape aerial hyphae/mycelia from agar; grind in 0.5 ml water in small tube to obtain creamy homogenate; add ~0.5 ml sterile skim milk; stir gently
3. Pipette suspension over silica gel, drop by drop; agitate briefly to distribute; place in ice-water bath 15 minutes
4. After 1 day at room temperature (particles appear dry), seal against moisture with Parafilm; store at 5°C or –20°C in moisture-proof box

**Retrieval:** Scatter a few silica gel crystals onto agar plate. Can resample multiple times from same container.

### Freezing at –20° to –80°C (Mechanical Freezer)

**Best for:** Most fungi; a good option when liquid nitrogen is unavailable.

**Duration:** Mitosporic ascomycetes, zygomycetes, yeasts: 5% failure rate after 5 years at –20°C. Medically important fungi, aerobic actinomycetes, algae: 2.3% failure rate over 6 months to 13 years at –70°C.

**Protocol (Ito 1991; Ito & Yokoyama 1983):**
1. Cut six 6-mm disks aseptically from vigorously growing culture
2. Place in sterile cryotubes containing 10% glycerol in water
3. Freeze at –80°C

**Duration:** Up to 5 years.

**Caution:** Do NOT repeatedly freeze and thaw — significantly reduces viability.

**Cryoprotectants used for mechanical freezing:**
- **Glycerol (10%)** — penetrating agent; protects intracellularly and extracellularly
- **DMSO (5%)** — penetrating agent; very effective but can be toxic

**Alternative protocol (using spore/mycelial suspension):**
1. Flood culture surface with 10% glycerol or 5% DMSO; gently scrape with pipette
2. Transfer suspension (0.5 ml aliquots) to sterile 2-ml screw-cap polypropylene cryovials
3. Freeze at –80°C

---

### Liquid Nitrogen Cryopreservation (–196°C) ★★★ Recommended ★★★

**Best for:** All fungi; particularly valuable for non-sporulating fungi, fungi with large/delicate spores that won't survive lyophilization, genetic stocks, dangerous pathogens, and oomycetes.

**Duration:** Permanent (cells in metabolic arrest at ≤–135°C; ice crystals do not grow below –139°C).

**Cryoprotectants:**
- **Glycerol (10%)** — penetrating agent; most effective for fungi
- **DMSO (5–10%)** — penetrating agent; very effective; toxic to some cells at room temperature
- Non-penetrating agents (sucrose, dextran, polyvinyl-pyrrolidone): external protection only; less commonly used alone

**Protocol A (non-sporulating or deep-growing mycelia):**
1. Fill sterile 2-ml screw-cap polypropylene cryovials with 0.5–1.0 ml sterile 10% glycerol
2. Cut 4-mm plugs from vigorously growing cultures using sterilized plastic straw
3. Place several plugs in vial; tighten cap; immediately transfer to vapor phase of liquid-nitrogen tank (~–170°C)
4. Label cryovials with cryoresistant ink marker; record location in freezer database

**Protocol B (sporulating fungi):**
1. Flood colony surface on agar slant with 10% glycerol or 5% DMSO; gently scrape with pipette to produce suspension
2. Transfer 0.5 ml aliquots to sterile 2-ml screw-cap polypropylene cryovials

**Protocol C (liquid-culture mycelia):**
1. Fragment broth culture for a few seconds in sterile mini-blender
2. Mix with equal parts 20% glycerol (final: 10%) or 10% DMSO (final: 5%)
3. Transfer to cryovials as above

**General Controlled-Freezing Protocol:**
1. Load filled vials into prelabeled cans in racks in freezing chamber of programmable freezer
2. Equilibrate at 4°C for ~10 minutes
3. Cool from 4° to –40°C at **1°C per minute**
4. Cool from –40° to –90°C at **10°C per minute**
5. Immediately transfer vials to liquid-nitrogen vapor at –150° to –180°C
6. (Do NOT immerse polypropylene vials in liquid nitrogen)

**Retrieval:** Remove vials from freezer; rapidly thaw in 37–55°C water bath until last trace of ice dissipates (glass ampoules/straws: <1 minute; polypropylene containers: longer); transfer aseptically to appropriate growth medium; check viability 2–7 days after thawing.

**Critical safety notes:**
- Store in vapor phase, not liquid phase (liquid-filled tubes will explode if quick-thawed)
- Vials must be sealed carefully to prevent entry of liquid nitrogen
- Maintain backup off-site liquid-nitrogen storage
- Replenish liquid nitrogen every ~2 days

**Advantages:** Prevents increased genetic variability; reduces labor compared to living stocks; eliminates need for repeated pathogenicity tests; prevents culture loss from contamination; ensures long-term availability.

### Lyophilization (Freeze-Drying) ★★★ Recommended for Sporulating Fungi ★★★

**Best for:** Species forming numerous small propagules (≤10 µm diameter). Especially good for spore-forming ascomycetes and their mitosporic forms. Also effective for dictyostelids (30-year viability; Raper 1984).

**Not suitable for:** Fungi with large/delicate spores; oomycetes; many basidiomycetes. (Exception: basidiomycetes can be lyophilized in the presence of trehalose — Croan 2000.)

**Duration:** Permanent (30+ years for some taxa).

**Equipment required:** High-quality mechanical vacuum pump, vacuum gauge, vacuum manifold, cold trap, insulated bath (dry ice + ethylene glycol), oxygen-gas torch, oxygen supply, 10-cm lengths of 6-mm soft glass tubing (heat-sealed at one end), or lyophilization ampoules, cotton, Pasteur pipettes.

**Menstrua (cryoprotective carriers):**
- Nonfat dry milk powder (sterile 5% or 10% solution) — most common
- Filter-sterilized bovine serum — also effective

**Protocol:**
1. Inoculate agar slant (medium supporting good growth and sporulation); allow to grow to resting phase (cultures too young or old will have very low post-lyophilization viability)
2. Prepare and label ≥5 lyophilization tubes
3. Add 1.5–2.0 ml sterile menstruum to agar slant; suspend spores by gently scraping with Pasteur pipette (if sporulation was light, transfer menstruum-spore mixture to second slant to combine)
4. Pipette ~200 µl spore suspension to each tube; plug loosely with cotton; lubricate open end with castor oil; place on vacuum manifold
5. Lower manifold until tubes are immersed in dry ice/ethylene glycol bath (–40° to –50°C); allow contents to freeze (~5 minutes)
6. Apply vacuum for ~30 minutes while bath warms to ~0°C
7. Raise manifold to remove tubes from solvent bath; continue drying at room temperature until pressure reaches ~30 milliTorr (evaporative cooling keeps samples frozen)
8. Seal tubes under vacuum using gas-oxygen torch
9. Store finished ampoules in numbered plastic boxes or sealed plastic bags at 4°C
10. Check purity and viability of one ampoule 1–2 weeks after preservation

**Viability categories:**
- Poor: 10–50 colonies/ampoule
- Moderate: 50–100 colonies/ampoule
- Good: 100–1000 colonies/ampoule

---

### Sclerotia and Resting Structures

**Best for:** Fungi that naturally produce sclerotia or long-term survival propagules in culture (Magnaporthe, Phymatotrichum, Cylindrocladium, many soil fungi).

**Protocol (Physarum species; Jump 1954):**
1. Place sterile cellophane cut to Petri dish dimensions over dish containing 1% water agar
2. Transfer actively growing plasmodium to cellophane; allow to grow overnight
3. Remove cellophane from agar; place in sterile dry Petri dish; cover; allow to dry 24 hours
4. Remove lid; air-dry until brittle
5. Cut cellophane into small pieces; store each in its own screw-cap vial

**Duration:** Myxomycete sclerotia/spherules: 1–3 years. Many soil fungal sclerotia: 2–5 years.

## Records and Record Keeping

Each strain in a culture collection should be recorded with these fields:
1. Strain number / accession number
2. Genus, species, variety, taxonomic authority
3. Required growth conditions
4. Date of isolation
5. Geographic locale of isolation (coordinates, elevation)
6. Isolation substrate or host
7. Person who isolated the culture
8. Provenance of the strain (full transfer history)
9. Isolate numbers in other collections for the same strain
10. Person who identified and re-identified the strain
11. Subjective synonyms
12. Comments

Additional useful fields: published references to the isolate, metabolites produced, transformations. Record of preservation vials (number of replicates, dates prepared, viability test results) and distribution log.

## Distribution and Shipping Regulations

### Domestic (US) Regulations

**Key agencies:**
- **USPHS (CDC):** Hazardous organisms (etiological agents) — permit CDC0.753 required for importation; Class I–IV classification; packaging per 42 CFR Part 72
- **USDA/APHIS:** Plant pathogens — PPQ Form 526 required for interstate/international movement; extremely virulent livestock pathogens require Form VS 16-3
- **DOT:** Safety aspects of transportation — 49 CFR Parts 171–178

**USPHS Hazard Classifications:**
- Class I: No recognized hazard; no special packaging required
- Class II: Ordinary potential hazard; distribution restricted to bona fide labs; special packaging required
- Class III: Special hazards; special packaging + letter from receiving institution required
- Class IV: Potentially dangerous to human health; USPHS and/or USDA permit required for shipping

### Packaging Requirements (domestic)

**Non-pathogenic cultures (≤50 ml):**
- Place in foam insert → fiberboard cylinder (for freight: cylinder → corrugated box)
- Primary container must be securely sealed and watertight
- Place inside secondary durable, watertight container
- Sufficient absorbent material between primary and secondary containers

**If more than one primary container in secondary container:** enough absorbent material to absorb all contents if primary containers break.

**For quantities >50 ml:** Shock-absorbent material between secondary and outer container required.

**Dry ice shipping:** Place dry ice outside secondary container (not inside); vent outer container to allow CO₂ to escape; do not ship dry ice via international mail (freight carrier required).

**Liquid nitrogen containers:** Must withstand ultra-low temperatures; follow DOT 49 CFR Part 173.316.

### International Shipping

- **IATA regulations** (International Air Transport Association) must be followed for air shipments worldwide
- **All foreign shipments** require Shipper's Export Declaration (DOC Form 7525-V) regardless of hazard
- Etiological agents must carry IATA infectious substance label and Shipper's Declaration for Dangerous Goods; labeled as UN 2814 Class 6.2
- Only volumes ≤50 ml of etiological agents may be sent by registered airmail; >50 ml requires specially tested containers and cargo aircraft only (DOT 49 CFR 173.387)
- **Canada:** Agriculture Canada requires annual import permit for all plant/animal pathogens; all human pathogens (Risk Groups II–IV) require Health Canada permit; extremely hazardous agents require Canadian Department of Transport Emergency Response Assistance Plan
- **Note:** Only Federal Express will carry materials labeled "infectious substances" to Canada under IATA regulations (at the time of publication)
